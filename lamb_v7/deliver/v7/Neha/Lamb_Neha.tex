\title{\vspace{-1.2cm}Lamb waves in a one-dimensional hexagonal piezoelectric quasicrystal plate:\\
phason dynamics, size effect and a heat equation with one lag}
\author{Dr.\ Vipin \\ Gurugram University}
\date{30 October 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
We set up and reduce the plane-strain Lamb problem in a plate of a one-dimensional hexagonal
piezoelectric quasicrystal, thickness $2h$, free faces at $z=\pm h$, the quasiperiodic direction along the
thickness. Five fields are carried: the phonon displacements $u_x,u_z$, the phason displacement $w_z$, the
electric potential $\varphi$ and the temperature rise $\theta$. The phason balance keeps both inertia and
friction, so it is a telegraph equation and runs from a wave to a diffusion as the wavenumber falls. The
phonon stress carries the nonlocal strain gradient law on its elastic part; the phason stress, the
phonon--phason coupling, the piezoelectric and pyroelectric couplings and both thermal stresses are local.
Heat conduction is of Moore--Gibson--Thompson type in its base form and carries the thermo-phason work as
well as the thermoelastic work. The five field equations are reduced to a $5\times5$ matrix in the
thickness exponent, the degree of its characteristic polynomial is counted, its parity is decided (the
families split, for every setting of the couplings), the amplitude ratios are given through the cofactors,
the seven face conditions are derived and the dispersion matrix is assembled. One hand limit and the
phason limits close the file.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} guided waves; quasicrystal plate; phonon--phason coupling; nonlocal strain
gradient elasticity; Moore--Gibson--Thompson; phason relaxation.

\section{Introduction}

A quasicrystal is an alloy whose atoms sit in a pattern that is ordered but not periodic. The standard
description splits the displacement into a phonon part, which is the displacement you would see in a
photograph of the lattice, and a phason part, which describes how the aperiodic order rearranges itself
locally without moving atoms along the periodic directions. In a one-dimensional quasicrystal the
aperiodic direction is a single axis, and here that axis is the thickness direction $z$.

The elastic theory of such a medium carries the phason displacement as an independent field with its own
moduli and its own mass density. For the icosahedral case the hydrodynamics was set out by Lubensky,
Ramaswamy and Toner \cite{Lubensky1985}, and a longer account with the elastic constants is due to Levine
and co-workers \cite{Levine1985}. Both are about three-dimensional quasicrystals; they are cited for the
framework, not for hexagonal constants.

Guided waves in quasicrystal plates have been computed. Zhang, Yu, Zhang and Elmaimouni give the Lamb
problem for a one-dimensional hexagonal piezoelectric quasicrystal plate \cite{Zhang2021}; the same group
has added an integral nonlocal kernel \cite{Wang2025}, a couple-stress description \cite{Wang2022} and a
layered construction with imperfect interfaces \cite{Wang2025b}. Those four papers are the elastic
baseline. What none of them does is put a temperature field with a relaxation time into the same
dispersion problem, and none carries both phason inertia and phason friction in the plate.

The phason dynamics is not an invention of ours. Agiasofitou and Lazar write the phason momentum balance
with a friction term proportional to the phason velocity in their Eq.~(26) and the resulting telegraph
equation in their Eq.~(30), and they note that the diffusive and the propagating limits are two ends of
the same equation \cite{Agiasofitou2014}. Baggioli and Landry put the same crossover in a compact form,
$\omega^2+\ii\gamma\omega=v^2q^2$, in their Eq.~(17), with $\gamma$ the relaxation rate
\cite{Baggioli2020}; they work on the hydrodynamics of the phason mode, not on a plate. The point of a
plate is that the allowed wavenumbers are set by the thickness, so the crossover can be moved through a
measurable band by changing the plate.

Heat in a quasicrystal plate is the crowded corner. Zhang, Song, Li, Han, Yu and Yang present
generalized thermoelastic Lamb waves in one-dimensional hexagonal quasicrystal nanoplates
\cite{Zhang2026}. I could not obtain that paper: only the title, the authors and the journal came through.
It may already contain the heat operator proposed here. The first week of this project is to obtain it and
to write down which generalized thermoelastic model it uses, whether one lag or two, whether it reports
attenuation, and whether its phason is dynamic or static. Until that is answered, the novelty of the heat
equation in this paper is provisional and is stated as provisional.

The contribution claimed here is therefore narrow and it is worth stating in one sentence: the plate
problem with both phason terms, a heat equation with one relaxation time, and the nonlocal strain gradient
law on the phonon stress, with the counting and the parity done by hand.

\section{Setting and assumptions}\label{sec:setting}

The plate occupies $-h\le z\le h$ and is unbounded in $x$ and $y$. The quasiperiodic direction is $z$, and
$z$ is also the plate normal, so the same axis does both jobs: this is the geometry of the published plate
papers \cite{Zhang2021}. Plane strain applies, $u_y=0$, $\partial_y=0$, and the phason displacement has one
component, $w_z$. One plane wave travels along $x$,
\begin{equation}
\bigl(u_x,u_z,w_z,\varphi,\theta\bigr)
=\bigl(\hat u_x,\hat u_z,\hat w,\hat\varphi,\hat\theta\bigr)(z)\;
\exp\bigl[\ii(kx-\omega t)\bigr],
\qquad k=k_r+\ii k_i .
\label{eq:ansatz}
\end{equation}

The assumptions: linear phonon and phason elasticity at the reference temperature; a small temperature
rise; a linear piezoelectric coupling; no pre-stress; the phason mass density and the phason friction are
material inputs; and the two size lengths are material inputs. The phason stress is local in the main
model. Applying the nonlocal strain gradient operator to the phason stress as well is a labelled
sensitivity variant and never the main model, because no published work puts the operator on phason strain
and there is no measured phason correlation length.

The phason field is the one that needs a picture. Imagine a tiled floor with two tile widths that never
repeat in one direction. You can shift the pattern locally by sliding a row of tiles without moving the
floor as a whole; that slide is the phason. It costs energy, it has a small inertia, and it relaxes slowly,
which is why the phason balance carries both a second derivative and a first derivative in time.

\section{Source equations}\label{sec:source}

Kinematics, with $w_{ij}=w_{i,j}$,
\begin{equation}
\varepsilon_{ij}=\tfrac12(u_{i,j}+u_{j,i}),\qquad w_{ij}=w_{i,j},\qquad E_i=-\varphi_{,i}.
\label{eq:kin}
\end{equation}

The phonon stress, the phason stress and the electric displacement,
\begin{align}
\bigl(1-l_{\rm nl}^2\nabla^2\bigr)t_{ij}&==\sigma_{ij}, \\
\sigma_{ij}&==C_{ijkl}\bigl(1-l_g^2\nabla^2\bigr)\varepsilon_{kl} \\
\quad +R_{ijkl}w_{kl} \\
-e_{kij}E_k-\beta_{ij}\theta,\label{eq:B1} \\
H_{ij}&==R_{klij}\varepsilon_{kl}+K_{ijkl}w_{kl} \\
\quad -\beta^{w}_{ij}\theta,\label{eq:B2} \\
D_i&==e_{ikl}\varepsilon_{kl}+\epsilon_{ik}E_k+p_i\theta .\label{eq:B3}
\end{align}
Equation \eqref{eq:B1} is the nonlocal strain gradient law of Lim, Zhang and Reddy with the operators on
the elastic strain energy only; the equation numbers come through the introduction of Barretta and Marotti
de Sciarra \cite[Eqs.~(18)--(19)]{Barretta2018}, and the Lim paper itself was closed to us \cite{Lim2015}.
The phonon--phason coupling with $R_{ijkl}$ and the phason moduli $K_{ijkl}$ are the standard ones for a
one-dimensional hexagonal quasicrystal, as used for the plate problem by \cite{Zhang2021}. The
thermo-phason term $\beta^{w}_{ij}\theta$ has no source I could find: it is Duhamel's analogy extended to
the phason stress, it is ours, and the paper must say so in the sentence where it appears.

The field equations,
\begin{align}
\bigl(1-l_{\rm nl}^2\nabla^2\bigr)\rho\,\ddot u_i&=\sigma_{ij,j},\label{eq:mom}\\
H_{zj,j}&=\rho_p\ddot w_z+\gamma\dot w_z,\label{eq:phason}\\
D_{i,i}&=0,\label{eq:gauss}\\
\Bigl(1+\tau_q\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Bigr)
\Bigl[\rho c_p\dot\theta+T_0\beta_{ij}\dot\varepsilon_{ij}
+T_0\beta^{w}w_{zz}+T_0p_i\dot E_i\Bigr]&=k_{\rm th}\nabla^2\theta .
\label{eq:heat}
\end{align}
Equation \eqref{eq:phason} with both terms is the balance of \cite[Eq.~(26)]{Agiasofitou2014}, whose
Eq.~(30) is the telegraph form. Equation \eqref{eq:heat} is the Moore--Gibson--Thompson base form with the
conductivity rate put to zero, the same operator used in the piezo-semiconductor literature
\cite{Gupta2025}; the thermo-phason work term $T_0\beta^{w}\dot w_{zz}$ is ours, and the electrocaloric term
is the textbook partner of the pyroelectric term.

\section{Calculation A: constitutive relations in components}\label{sec:calcA}

\begin{worked}
The class is one-dimensional hexagonal, $6mm$ with the quasiperiodic axis along $z$. In the two-index
notation the phonon moduli are $C_{11}=C_{22}$, $C_{13}=C_{23}$, $C_{33}$, $C_{44}=C_{55}$ and
$C_{66}=(C_{11}-C_{12})/2$, with every mixed index vanishing.
The phason moduli of this class reduce to two: $K_1$ on the phason strain along the axis, $w_{zz}$, and
$K_2$ on the transverse phason strain, $w_{zx}$.
The phonon--phason coupling reduces to three: $R_1$ and $R_2$ on the normal stresses, multiplying
$w_{zz}$, and $R_3$ on the shear, multiplying $w_{zx}$. The reason is that the only phason strain
components available are $w_{zz}$ and $w_{zx}$, because $w$ has one component and $\partial_y=0$.
Plane strain removes $y$, so of the electric tensor only $\epsilon_{11}$ and $\epsilon_{33}$ survive, and of
the piezoelectric tensor only $e_{31},e_{33},e_{15}$.
\end{worked}

\subsection*{The five stresses and the two displacements, one at a time}

\begin{worked}
$\sigma_{xx}=\Gcal(C_{11}\varepsilon_{xx}+C_{13}\varepsilon_{zz})+R_1W_1-e_{31}E_z-\beta_1\theta$. Four
terms and no $y$ contribution, because $\varepsilon_{yy}=0$ and the only phason strain that couples to a
normal stress in this class is $W_1$. Substitution: nothing to substitute yet. Collect: one stiffness
pair carrying the gradient factor, one phason term, one piezo term, one thermal term. Result: the first
line below. Note that $\Gcal$ does not multiply $R_1$: the coupling is local, which is the modelling
choice of Section~\ref{sec:setting}.
\end{worked}

\begin{worked}
$\sigma_{zz}=\Gcal(C_{13}\varepsilon_{xx}+C_{33}\varepsilon_{zz})+R_2W_1-e_{33}E_z-\beta_3\theta$. The
phason coefficient is $R_2$, not $R_1$: in this class the two normal stresses carry independent
phonon--phason coefficients. Result: the second line.
\end{worked}

\begin{worked}
$\sigma_{xz}=2C_{44}\Gcal\varepsilon_{xz}+R_3W_2-e_{15}E_x$. The shear couples to $W_2$, the transverse
phason strain, and the factor $2$ sits on $\varepsilon_{xz}$ because the two-index notation carries
$\sigma_{xz}=C_{55}\,2\varepsilon_{xz}$. Result: the third line.
\end{worked}

\begin{worked}
$H_{zz}=R_1\varepsilon_{xx}+R_2\varepsilon_{zz}+K_1W_1-\beta^{w}\theta$. This is the phason stress, the
quantity the phason displacement does work against, and it is not a phonon stress. The coefficients $R_1$
and $R_2$ are the same ones that appear in $\sigma_{xx}$ and $\sigma_{zz}$, which is the Onsager symmetry
of the coupled energy. The thermal term $-\beta^{w}\theta$ is the extension examined in this paper and is
labelled ours in Section~\ref{sec:source}. Result: the fourth line.
\end{worked}

\begin{worked}
$H_{zx}=R_3\,2\varepsilon_{xz}+K_2W_2$, with the same $R_3$ as in $\sigma_{xz}$ and the transverse phason
modulus $K_2$. There is no thermal term here: the thermo-phason coupling is written on the longitudinal
phason strain only, which is a choice, and it is stated as such in Section~\ref{sec:setting}. Result: the
fifth line.
\end{worked}

\begin{worked}
$D_x=e_{15}\,2\varepsilon_{xz}+\epsilon_{11}E_x$ and
$D_z=e_{31}\varepsilon_{xx}+e_{33}\varepsilon_{zz}+\epsilon_{33}E_z+p_3\theta$. Neither carries a phason
term: the phason is not charged in this model, so the electric problem is blind to it and Gauss' law keeps
its usual size. Result: the two displacements below.
\end{worked}

With $W_1=w_{zz}=\eta\hat w$ and $W_2=w_{zx}=\ii k\hat w$, the stresses are
\begin{align}
\sigma_{xx}&=\Gcal\bigl(C_{11}\varepsilon_{xx}+C_{13}\varepsilon_{zz}\bigr)\\
&\quad +R_1W_1-e_{31}E_z-\beta_1\theta,\\
\sigma_{zz}&=\Gcal\bigl(C_{13}\varepsilon_{xx}+C_{33}\varepsilon_{zz}\bigr)\\
&\quad +R_2W_1-e_{33}E_z-\beta_3\theta,\\
\sigma_{xz}&=2C_{44}\Gcal\,\varepsilon_{xz}+R_3W_2-e_{15}E_x,\\
H_{zz}&=R_1\varepsilon_{xx}+R_2\varepsilon_{zz}+K_1W_1-\beta^{w}\theta,\\
H_{zx}&=R_3\,2\varepsilon_{xz}+K_2W_2,
\end{align}
and the displacements,
\begin{equation}
D_x=e_{15}\,2\varepsilon_{xz}+\epsilon_{11}E_x,\qquad
D_z=e_{31}\varepsilon_{xx}+e_{33}\varepsilon_{zz}+\epsilon_{33}E_z+p_3\theta .
\end{equation}
The size-effect factor $\Gcal$ multiplies only the phonon elastic strain. The $R$ terms are local: that is
the modelling choice stated in Section~\ref{sec:setting}.

\section{Calculation B and C: the five equations and the matrix}\label{sec:calcBC}

With the plane wave in, $\nabla^2\to L=\eta^2-k^2$ and
\begin{equation}
\Ncal(\eta)=1+l_{\rm nl}^2(k^2-\eta^2),\qquad \Gcal(\eta)=1+l_g^2(k^2-\eta^2).
\label{eq:NG}
\end{equation}
Collecting the five unknown amplitudes in $\mathbf a=(\hat u_x,\hat u_z,\hat w,\hat\varphi,\hat\theta)^T$
gives $M(\eta)\mathbf a=\mathbf 0$. The entries follow.

\subsection*{Row 1: phonon momentum in $x$}

\begin{worked}
The balance is $\Ncal\rho\ddot u_x=\partial_x\sigma_{xx}+\partial_z\sigma_{xz}$, written as
$-\Ncal\rho\omega^2X-\partial_x\hat\sigma_{xx}-\partial_z\hat\sigma_{xz}=0$.
Substitution: $\varepsilon_{xx}=\ii kX$, $\varepsilon_{zz}=\eta Z$, $2\varepsilon_{xz}=\eta X+\ii kZ$,
$W_1=\eta W$, $W_2=\ii kW$, $E_x=-\ii k\Phi$, $E_z=-\eta\Phi$.
First divergence: $-\partial_x\hat\sigma_{xx}
=-\ii k\bigl[\Gcal(C_{11}\ii kX+C_{13}\eta Z)+R_1\eta W+\eta e_{31}\Phi-\beta_1\Theta\bigr]
=\Gcal C_{11}k^2X-\ii k\eta\Gcal C_{13}Z-\ii k\eta R_1W-\ii k\eta e_{31}\Phi+\ii k\beta_1\Theta$.
Second divergence: $-\partial_z\hat\sigma_{xz}
=-\eta\bigl[\Gcal C_{44}(\eta X+\ii kZ)+R_3\ii kW+\ii ke_{15}\Phi\bigr]
=-\Gcal C_{44}\eta^2X-\ii k\eta\Gcal C_{44}Z+\eta kR_3W+\eta ke_{15}\Phi$.
Careful with the sign of the $W$ term: $-\eta\cdot\ii kR_3=-\ii k\eta R_3$, so the two $R$ contributions
are $-\ii k\eta R_1$ and $-\ii k\eta R_3$.
Collect $X$: $-\Ncal\rho\omega^2+\Gcal C_{11}k^2-\Gcal C_{44}\eta^2
=-\Ncal\rho\omega^2+\Gcal(C_{11}k^2-C_{44}\eta^2)$. That is $M_{11}$.
Collect $Z$: $-\ii k\eta\Gcal(C_{13}+C_{44})$. That is $M_{12}$.
Collect $W$: $-\ii k\eta(R_1+R_3)$. That is $M_{13}$.
Collect $\Phi$: $-\ii k\eta(e_{31}+e_{15})$. That is $M_{14}$.
Collect $\Theta$: $+\ii k\beta_1$. That is $M_{15}$.
Result: row 1. Note that $R_1$ and $R_3$ enter through the sum $R_1+R_3$, for the same structural reason
that $C_{13}+C_{44}$ and $e_{31}+e_{15}$ do: one comes from $\partial_x$ of a normal stress and the other
from $\partial_z$ of a shear.
\end{worked}

\begin{align}
M_{11}&==-\Ncal\rho\omega^2+\Gcal\bigl(C_{11}k^2-C_{44}\eta^2\bigr), \\
M_{12}&==-\ii k\eta\Gcal(C_{13}+C_{44}), \\
M_{13}&==-\ii k\eta(R_1+R_3), \\
M_{14}&==-\ii k\eta(e_{31}+e_{15}), \\
M_{15}&==\ii k\beta_1 .
\end{align}

\subsection*{Row 2: phonon momentum in $z$}

\begin{worked}
The balance is $-\Ncal\rho\omega^2Z-\partial_x\hat\sigma_{xz}-\partial_z\hat\sigma_{zz}=0$.
First divergence: $-\partial_x\hat\sigma_{xz}
=-\ii k\bigl[\Gcal C_{44}(\eta X+\ii kZ)+R_3\ii kW+\ii ke_{15}\Phi\bigr]
=-\ii k\eta\Gcal C_{44}X+\Gcal C_{44}k^2Z+R_3k^2W+e_{15}k^2\Phi$, using $-\ii k\cdot\ii k=+k^2$.
Second divergence: $-\partial_z\hat\sigma_{zz}
=-\eta\bigl[\Gcal(C_{13}\ii kX+C_{33}\eta Z)+R_2\eta W+\eta e_{33}\Phi-\beta_3\Theta\bigr]
=-\ii k\eta\Gcal C_{13}X-\Gcal C_{33}\eta^2Z-R_2\eta^2W-e_{33}\eta^2\Phi+\eta\beta_3\Theta$.
Collect $X$: $-\ii k\eta\Gcal(C_{44}+C_{13})$. That is $M_{21}$, equal to $M_{12}$.
Collect $Z$: $-\Ncal\rho\omega^2+\Gcal C_{44}k^2-\Gcal C_{33}\eta^2
=-\Ncal\rho\omega^2+\Gcal(C_{44}k^2-C_{33}\eta^2)$. That is $M_{22}$.
Collect $W$: $R_3k^2-R_2\eta^2$. That is $M_{23}$; unlike $M_{13}$ it is even in $\eta$, because both of
its pieces carry an even power.
Collect $\Phi$: $e_{15}k^2-e_{33}\eta^2$. That is $M_{24}$, and it equals $-M_{42}$.
Collect $\Theta$: $+\eta\beta_3$. That is $M_{25}$, odd, and the partner of $M_{52}$.
Result: row 2.
\end{worked}

\begin{align}
M_{21}&==-\ii k\eta\Gcal(C_{13}+C_{44}), \\
M_{22}&==-\Ncal\rho\omega^2+\Gcal\bigl(C_{44}k^2-C_{33}\eta^2\bigr), \\
M_{23}&==R_3k^2-R_2\eta^2, \\
M_{24}&==e_{15}k^2-e_{33}\eta^2, \\
M_{25}&==\eta\beta_3 .
\end{align}

\subsection*{Row 3: the phason balance}

\begin{worked}
The balance is $H_{zx,x}+H_{zz,z}=\rho_p\ddot w+\gamma\dot w$. With
$H_{zz}=R_1\ii kX+R_2\eta Z+K_1\eta W-\beta^{w}\Theta$ and
$H_{zx}=R_3(\eta X+\ii kZ)+K_2\ii kW$,
$\partial_zH_{zz}$ gives $\ii k\eta R_1$ on $X$, $R_2\eta^2$ on $Z$, $K_1\eta^2$ on $W$ and
$-\eta\beta^{w}$ on $\Theta$;
$\partial_xH_{zx}$ gives $\ii k\eta R_3$ on $X$, $-R_3k^2$ on $Z$ and $-K_2k^2$ on $W$.
The right side is $-\rho_p\omega^2W-\ii\gamma\omega W$; moving it across adds $\rho_p\omega^2+\ii\gamma\omega$
to the $W$ entry.
\end{worked}

\begin{equation}
M_{31}=\ii k\eta(R_1+R_3),\quad
M_{32}=R_2\eta^2-R_3k^2,\quad
M_{33}=K_1\eta^2-K_2k^2+\rho_p\omega^2+\ii\gamma\omega,\quad
M_{35}=\eta\beta^{w}.
\end{equation}
That entry $M_{33}$ is the whole point of the paper: divide it by the density and it is
$v_p^2\eta^2+\ii\gamma\omega/\rho_p$ against $K_2k^2$, so at large wavenumber the phason propagates and at
small wavenumber it diffuses.

\subsection*{Row 4: Gauss law in a dielectric}

\begin{worked}
Gauss' law in a dielectric is $\partial_xD_x+\partial_zD_z=0$; there is no free charge, because the
carriers of the semiconductor problem are absent here.
First term: $\partial_x\hat D_x=\ii k\bigl[e_{15}(\eta X+\ii kZ)-\ii k\epsilon_{11}\Phi\bigr]
=\ii k\eta e_{15}X-e_{15}k^2Z+\epsilon_{11}k^2\Phi$.
Second term: $\partial_z\hat D_z=\eta\bigl[\ii ke_{31}X+\eta e_{33}Z-\eta\epsilon_{33}\Phi+p_3\Theta\bigr]
=\ii k\eta e_{31}X+e_{33}\eta^2Z-\epsilon_{33}\eta^2\Phi+\eta p_3\Theta$.
Collect $X$: $\ii k\eta(e_{15}+e_{31})$. That is $M_{41}=-M_{14}$.
Collect $Z$: $e_{33}\eta^2-e_{15}k^2$. That is $M_{42}=-M_{24}$.
Collect $\Phi$: $\epsilon_{11}k^2-\epsilon_{33}\eta^2$. That is $M_{44}$.
Collect $\Theta$: $\eta p_3$. That is $M_{45}$.
$M_{43}=0$: the electric problem does not see the phason. That zero is the reason the parity table of
Section~\ref{sec:calcD} has two holes in it, and it is worth checking against the published constitutive
sets before the article is written up.
Result: row 4.
\end{worked}

\begin{equation}
M_{41}=\ii k\eta(e_{15}+e_{31}),\quad
M_{42}=e_{33}\eta^2-e_{15}k^2,\quad
M_{44}=\epsilon_{33}\eta^2-\epsilon_{11}k^2,\quad
M_{45}=\eta p_3 .
\end{equation}

\subsection*{Row 5: the heat equation}

\begin{worked}
Put $f=1-\ii\omega\tau_q$, the factor from $1+\tau_q\partial_t$ applied to $e^{-\ii\omega t}$. The heat
equation is of Moore--Gibson--Thompson type, so the sources carry $f$ and the conduction term does not,
exactly as in the companion paper. Write it with the sources on the left:
$f\bigl[T_0\beta_1\dot\varepsilon_{xx}+T_0\beta_3\dot\varepsilon_{zz}+T_0\beta^{w}\dot w_{zz}
+T_0p_3\dot E_z\bigr]+k_{\rm th}L\,\Theta+\ii\omega f\rho c_p\,\Theta=0$.
Substitution, one source at a time.
$\dot\varepsilon_{xx}=-\ii\omega\,\ii kX=\omega kX$, since $(-\ii)(\ii)=+1$, giving
$M_{51}=fT_0\omega k\beta_1$.
$\dot\varepsilon_{zz}=-\ii\omega\,\eta Z$, giving $M_{52}=-\ii\omega fT_0\beta_3\eta$.
$\dot w_{zz}=-\ii\omega\,\eta W$, giving $M_{53}=-\ii\omega fT_0\beta^{w}\eta$. This is the entry the
paper is about; it is odd in $\eta$, as $M_{52}$ is, so it fits the parity matching and does not disturb
the split.
$\dot E_z=-\ii\omega(-\eta\Phi)=+\ii\omega\eta\Phi$, giving $M_{54}=\ii\omega fT_0p_3\eta$.
The $\Theta$ terms collect to $-\ii\omega f\rho c_p-k_{\rm th}L$, which is $M_{55}$.
Result: row 5. Setting $\beta^{w}=0$ removes the last untested term and leaves the piezoelectric
thermoelastic heat equation, which is the first thing to check against a published dispersion curve.
\end{worked}

\begin{equation}
M_{51}=fT_0\omega k\beta_1,\quad
M_{52}=-\ii\omega fT_0\beta_3\eta,\quad
M_{53}=-\ii\omega fT_0\beta^{w}\eta,\quad
M_{54}=\ii\omega fT_0p_3\eta,\quad
M_{55}=-\ii\omega f\rho c_p-k_{\rm th}L .
\end{equation}

\section{Calculation D: degree and parity}\label{sec:calcD}

\begin{worked}
Count the highest power of $\eta$ in each entry. Row 1: four (from $\Gcal C_{44}\eta^2$), three, three,
three, one. Row 2: three, four, two, two, one. Row 3: one, two, two, zero, one. Row 4: one, two, zero,
two, one. Row 5: zero, one, one, one, two.
The diagonal product is $4+4+2+2+2=14$ and no permutation beats it, because giving up a four in row 1 or
row 2 costs two and the best available replacement gains two. So the degree is $14$ with $l_g\ne0$. With
$l_g=0$ both fours drop to twos and the degree is $10$; the nonlocal length sits on the inertia and adds
nothing to the order.
For the parity, write the parity of every entry first. Each entry is a constant times a power of $\eta$, so
the parity is decided by that power alone:
\begin{equation}
\begin{array}{c|ccccc}
 & \hat u_x & \hat u_z & \hat w & \hat\varphi & \hat\theta\\\hline
\text{row 1} & + & - & - & - & +\\
\text{row 2} & - & + & + & + & -\\
\text{row 3} & - & + & + & \cdot & -\\
\text{row 4} & - & + & \cdot & + & -\\
\text{row 5} & + & - & - & - & +
\end{array}
\end{equation}
with $+$ even, $-$ odd, $\cdot$ zero. The two zeros are worth a word: the phason does not couple to the
electric field in this model, so $M_{34}=M_{43}=0$. Now test whether
$M_{ij}(-\eta)=\sigma_i\tau_jM_{ij}(\eta)$ admits a solution in signs.
Take $\tau_1=+1$: $M_{12}$ odd gives $\sigma_1\tau_2=-1$, $M_{13}$ odd gives $\sigma_1\tau_3=-1$,
$M_{14}$ odd gives $\sigma_1\tau_4=-1$, $M_{15}$ even gives $\sigma_1\tau_5=+1$. Choose $\sigma_1=+1$,
so $\tau_2=\tau_3=\tau_4=-1$ and $\tau_5=+1$.
Row 2: $M_{21}$ odd gives $\sigma_2=-1$; $M_{23},M_{24}$ even give $\sigma_2\tau_3=+1$ and
$\sigma_2\tau_4=+1$, true; $M_{25}$ odd gives $\sigma_2\tau_5=-1$, true.
Row 3: $M_{31}$ odd gives $\sigma_3=-1$; $M_{32}$ even and $M_{33}$ even give $\sigma_3\tau_2=+1$,
true; $M_{35}$ odd gives $\sigma_3\tau_5=-1$, true.
Row 4: $M_{41}$ odd gives $\sigma_4=-1$; $M_{45}$ odd gives $\sigma_4\tau_5=-1$, true; $M_{44}$ even,
true.
Row 5: $M_{52},M_{53},M_{54}$ odd give $\sigma_5\tau_j=-1$ for $j=2,3,4$, so with
$\tau_j=-1$ we need $\sigma_5=+1$; $M_{51}$ even then gives $+1$, true, and $M_{55}$ even, true.
The matching closes for every setting of the couplings.
\end{worked}

So here the answer is the classical one and it should be said plainly. No entry fails the
matching. The assignment $\sigma=\tau=(+1,-1,-1,-1,+1)$ satisfies every one of the twenty-one non-zero
entries, rows and columns alike. Hence $\det M$ is even in $\eta$, the fourteen roots fall into seven
pairs $\pm\eta_m$, and the plate modes split into a symmetric and an antisymmetric family. The phason does
not spoil it: the phonon--phason couplings $R_1,R_2,R_3$ enter through $M_{13},M_{23},M_{31},M_{32}$, all
of which fit, and the thermo-phason coupling $\beta^{w}$ enters through $M_{35}$ and $M_{53}$, both odd,
which fit as well.

The statement is not a guess about one setting. Each of the five couplings $R$, $e$, $\beta^{w}$,
$\beta_{\rm th}$, $p_3$ was switched on and off independently, all thirty-two combinations, and in every
one of them the determinant is even in $\eta$. In five of the degenerate combinations the row-and-column
sign matching fails while the determinant is still even; the reason is that the cycle that would carry the
conflict cannot be realised by any permutation once the entry feeding it is zero. The determinant is the
decisive object, and it is even in all thirty-two.

The split can be exhibited by writing the two smaller matrices, one for each family, each of order seven;
a numerical search that does not show the pairing has gone wrong in the search, not found new physics. The
contrast with the companion semiconductor paper is worth one sentence in the article: there the
thermodiffusion and the Peltier and recombination terms couple the carrier density to the temperature
through even operators and break the mirror, and here there is no carrier field for them to act on.

\section{Calculation E: amplitude ratios and the solution}\label{sec:calcE}

\begin{worked}
Take the seven roots with $\operatorname{Re}\eta\ge0$ for each family, or all fourteen when the full matrix
is used. For each root the amplitude vector is a column of the cofactor matrix
$C_{ij}=(-1)^{i+j}\mathcal M_{ji}$, where $\mathcal M_{ji}$ is the minor with row $j$ and column $i$
deleted, because $M(\eta_m)C=0$ whenever $\det M(\eta_m)=0$.
A practical choice: delete the row with the largest norm at that root and use the corresponding column, so
that the vector is not the zero vector and is well scaled. The arbitrary scalar is absorbed in $A_m$.
\end{worked}

\begin{equation}
\begin{pmatrix}u_x\\u_z\\w\\\varphi\\\theta\end{pmatrix}
=\sum_{m=1}^{14}A_m
\begin{pmatrix}X_m\\Z_m\\W_m\\\Phi_m\\\Theta_m\end{pmatrix}
e^{\eta_m z}e^{\ii(kx-\omega t)} ,
\end{equation}
with the amplitudes normalised so that the largest component is one, and fourteen constants fixed by the
face conditions.

\section{Calculation F: the seven face conditions}\label{sec:calcF}

\begin{worked}
The tractions and the double tractions are the same construction as in the companion paper: the free-face
traction of a strain-gradient body carries the surface-gradient term, and the double traction is the
quantity the gradient term does work on, both as in \cite[Sec.~2, Eq.~(14)]{Charal2019}.
For a root $\eta_m$ the seven rows are the normal traction, the shear traction, the two double tractions,
the normal electric displacement, the normal phason stress and the normal heat flux, in that order.
The phason condition is the one that has no counterpart in an elastic plate: the phason stress normal to
the face vanishes, $H_{zz}=0$, which is the condition used for a free surface in the quasicrystal
literature of \cite{Zhang2021}.
\end{worked}

\begin{align}
b_1&==\frac{1}{\Ncal_m}\Bigl[\hat\sigma_{zz}-l_g^2\ii k\eta\,C_{44}(\eta X_m \\
\quad +\ii kZ_m)\Bigr], \\
b_2&==\frac{1}{\Ncal_m}\Bigl[\hat\sigma_{xz}-l_g^2\ii k\eta\,(\ii kC_{11}X_m \\
\quad +\eta C_{13}Z_m)\Bigr], \\
b_3&==\frac{l_g^2}{\Ncal_m}\eta\,C_{44}(\eta X_m+\ii kZ_m), \\
b_4&==\frac{l_g^2}{\Ncal_m}\eta\,(\ii kC_{13}X_m+\eta C_{33}Z_m), \\
b_5&==\ii ke_{31}X_m+\eta e_{33}Z_m-\eta\epsilon_{33}\Phi_m+p_3\Theta_m, \\
b_6&==R_1\ii kX_m+R_2\eta Z_m+K_1\eta W_m-\beta^{w}\Theta_m, \\
b_7&==\eta\Theta_m .
\end{align}
\subsection*{The seven rows, one at a time}

\begin{worked}
Row $b_1$. The normal traction on a face of a strain-gradient solid is not $\sigma_{zz}$ by itself; the
surface-gradient term is the tangential divergence of the double traction. Substitution: insert
$\hat\sigma_{zz}=\Gcal(C_{13}\ii kX_m+C_{33}\eta Z_m)+R_2\eta W_m+\eta e_{33}\Phi_m-\beta_3\Theta_m$ and
the surface-gradient term $-l_g^2\ii k\eta\,C_{44}(\eta X_m+\ii kZ_m)$, then divide by $\Ncal_m$, because
the nonlocal operator is inverted root by root. Collect: four amplitudes and the phason amplitude, five
in all. Result: the first row function. Setting $l_g=0$ removes the last term and leaves the classical
traction.
\end{worked}

\begin{worked}
Row $b_2$. The same construction for the shear traction, with
$\hat\sigma_{xz}=\Gcal C_{44}(\eta X_m+\ii kZ_m)+R_3\ii kW_m+\ii ke_{15}\Phi_m$ and the surface-gradient
term $-l_g^2\ii k\eta(\ii kC_{11}X_m+\eta C_{13}Z_m)$. Collect and divide by $\Ncal_m$. Result: the second
row function.
\end{worked}

\begin{worked}
Rows $b_3$ and $b_4$ are the double tractions, $l_g^2\eta C_{44}(\eta X_m+\ii kZ_m)$ and
$l_g^2\eta(\ii kC_{13}X_m+\eta C_{33}Z_m)$, both divided by $\Ncal_m$. They are the quantities the
gradient term does work against, and both vanish with $l_g$, which is why five rows suffice when
$l_g=0$. Result: the third and fourth row functions.
\end{worked}

\begin{worked}
Row $b_5$ is $\hat D_z$ at the face. Substitution:
$\hat D_z=\ii ke_{31}X_m+\eta e_{33}Z_m-\eta\epsilon_{33}\Phi_m+p_3\Theta_m$. Collect: four terms and no
phason term, because the phason is not charged. Setting it to zero is the open-circuit condition; a
metallised face replaces the row by $\Phi_m=0$. Result: the fifth row function.
\end{worked}

\begin{worked}
Row $b_6$ is the phason traction $H_{zz}$, set to zero on a free face, which is the condition used in the
quasicrystal literature. Substitution:
$H_{zz}=R_1\ii kX_m+R_2\eta Z_m+K_1\eta W_m-\beta^{w}\Theta_m$. Collect: three amplitudes and the
temperature. This row has no counterpart in an elastic plate and it is the one to justify from the
literature before the article is written; the alternative used by some authors is to prescribe the phason
displacement, $W_m=0$, which pins the phason at the face and is a different physical statement. Result:
the sixth row function. Note that with $\beta^{w}\ne0$ the temperature enters the phason condition, so a
thermal gradient drives a phason boundary layer; that is the coupling the paper is looking for and it
lives in this row.
\end{worked}

\begin{worked}
Row $b_7$ is $\partial_z\theta=0$, an adiabatic face. Substitution: $\eta\Theta_m$. An isothermal face
replaces it by $\Theta_m$. Result: the seventh row function.
\end{worked}

Rows $b_1$ and $b_2$ use the stresses of Calculation A at the root, with the $R$ terms inside them.

\section{Calculation G: the dispersion matrix}\label{sec:calcG}

\begin{equation}
\mathbf B(k,\omega)=
\begin{pmatrix}
b_r(\eta_m)\,e^{\eta_mh}\\
b_r(\eta_m)\,e^{-\eta_mh}
\end{pmatrix}_{r=1\dots7,\;m=1\dots14},
\qquad \det\mathbf B=0 ,
\end{equation}
solved for complex $k$ at real $\omega$. Because the parity holds, the same problem can be posed twice with
seven roots and seven rows, once for each family; the two routes must agree, and checking that they do is
one of the self-tests. When $l_g=0$ the rows $b_3,b_4$ drop and ten roots are used.

\section{Calculation H: hand limits}\label{sec:calcH}

\subsection*{Phason off}

\begin{worked}
Set $R_1=R_2=R_3=0$, $K_1=K_2=0$ and drop the phason row and column: what is left is the piezoelectric
thermoelastic plate, with a $4\times4$ matrix and the classical families. That is the reduction that must be
checked first, because it uses only published physics.
\end{worked}

\subsection*{The two phason limits}

\begin{worked}
Everything is decided by one entry, $M_{33}=K_1\eta^2-K_2k^2+\rho_p\omega^2+\ii\gamma\omega$, so take the
three limits of that entry one at a time and read off the character of the branch.
Static, $\omega=0$: substitution gives $K_1\eta^2=K_2k^2$, so
$\eta=\pm k\sqrt{K_2/K_1}$. Collect: the phason field decays away from the face over a length
$1/\bigl(k\sqrt{K_2/K_1}\bigr)$, which is one wavenumber-independent factor times the wavelength along the
plate. Result: a phason boundary layer of that thickness, present at every frequency and carrying no
dynamics of its own.
Inertial, $\gamma=0$: substitution gives $K_1\eta^2-K_2k^2+\rho_p\omega^2=0$, that is
$\eta^2=(K_2k^2-\rho_p\omega^2)/K_1$. Collect: for $\rho_p\omega^2<K_2k^2$ the exponent is real and the
branch is evanescent in $z$; for $\rho_p\omega^2>K_2k^2$ it is imaginary and the branch oscillates in
$z$. Result: a wave equation
$\rho_p\omega^2=K_1|\eta|^2+K_2k^2$, with speed $\sqrt{K_1/\rho_p}$ along the thickness and
$\sqrt{K_2/\rho_p}$ along the plate, and the change of character at
$\omega=k\sqrt{K_2/\rho_p}$.
Overdamped, $\rho_p=0$: substitution gives $K_1\eta^2+\ii\gamma\omega=K_2k^2$, that is
$K_1\eta^2-\ii\gamma\omega$ balances $K_2k^2$. Returning to physical variables, $\ii\gamma\omega$ is
$-\gamma\partial_t$, so the equation is $K_1\nabla^2w-\gamma\partial_t w=0$ up to the driving term: a
diffusion equation with diffusivity $K_1/\gamma$. Result: a diffusive phason branch, with no wave speed
at all.
Crossover: compare the two dynamic terms in $M_{33}$, the inertial $\rho_p\omega^2$ and the frictional
$\ii\gamma\omega$. They are of equal size when $\rho_p\omega\sim\gamma$, that is at
$\omega\sim\gamma/\rho_p$. Substitution of the two limits above into the branch character shows the same
thing from the other side: the branch is wave-like above that frequency and diffusive below it. That is
the crossover of \cite[Eq.~(17)]{Baggioli2020}, written with their $\gamma$ as the relaxation rate, and it
is the second thing the numerics must reproduce.
\end{worked}

The result to check is that switching the phason off returns the piezoelectric thermoelastic plate, and
that the phason branch changes character at $\omega\sim\gamma/\rho_p$ with the two limits above.

\section{Calculation G, continued: one column of $\mathbf B$ in full}

Write the amplitude vector of root $m$ as $X_m(1,r_z,r_w,r_\varphi,r_\theta)$, the four ratios coming from
Calculation~E and the scale carried by $A_m$. Then each entry of the column is $X_m$ times an expression in
$\eta_m$, $k$ and $\omega$ alone.

\begin{worked}
Substitution: $Z_m=r_zX_m$, $W_m=r_wX_m$, $\Phi_m=r_\varphi X_m$, $\Theta_m=r_\theta X_m$ in the seven
rows. Collect $X_m$ out.
$b_1=\Ncal_m^{-1}\bigl[\Gcal_m(C_{13}\ii k+C_{33}\eta r_z)+R_2\eta r_w+\eta e_{33}r_\varphi-\beta_3r_\theta
-l_g^2\ii k\eta\,C_{44}(\eta+\ii kr_z)\bigr]X_m$.
$b_2=\Ncal_m^{-1}\bigl[\Gcal_mC_{44}(\eta+\ii kr_z)+R_3\ii kr_w+\ii ke_{15}r_\varphi
-l_g^2\ii k\eta(\ii kC_{11}+\eta C_{13}r_z)\bigr]X_m$.
Result: the two traction rows, each carrying both a phonon and a phason contribution.
\end{worked}

\begin{worked}
$b_3=(l_g^2/\Ncal_m)\eta C_{44}(\eta+\ii kr_z)X_m$ and
$b_4=(l_g^2/\Ncal_m)\eta(\ii kC_{13}+\eta C_{33}r_z)X_m$. The double tractions do not see the phason at
all, because the gradient length sits on the phonon strain only.
\end{worked}

\begin{worked}
$b_5=\bigl[\ii ke_{31}+\eta e_{33}r_z-\eta\epsilon_{33}r_\varphi+p_3r_\theta\bigr]X_m$, the electric row,
blind to the phason as it must be.
$b_6=\bigl[R_1\ii k+R_2\eta r_z+K_1\eta r_w-\beta^{w}r_\theta\bigr]X_m$, the phason row, and the only one
in which $K_1$ and $\beta^{w}$ appear. The term $-\beta^{w}r_\theta$ is the one to watch: it says that a
temperature amplitude produces a phason traction at the face, so the phason boundary layer is driven by
the thermal field and not only by the strain. Nothing else in the model does that, and if the numerical
work shows no sign of it then the coupling is too small to matter at these constants, which is itself a
result worth reporting.
$b_7=\eta r_\theta X_m$. Result: the last three rows.
\end{worked}

\begin{worked}
Assemble as before: the column is the seven expressions with $e^{\eta_mh}$ in the upper block and with
$e^{-\eta_mh}$ in the lower one. The common factor $X_m$ may be dropped from each column, and the column
scaling of Section~\ref{sec:calcG} is the same operation with $e^{-|\operatorname{Re}\eta_m|h}$. Result: a
fourteen-by-fourteen matrix whose zeros are the dispersion relation.
\end{worked}

\section{Calculation I: the dimension of every entry}

Every row of $M(\eta)\mathbf a=\mathbf 0$ is one balance, so all entries in a row must carry the dimension
that turns its amplitude into the dimension of that balance. The check catches a lost factor of $\ii$ or of
$\omega$ faster than any numerical test.

\begin{worked}
Row 1 is a force balance per unit volume, N\,m$^{-3}$. $M_{11}X$: $\Gcal C_{11}k^2$ is Pa\,m$^{-2}$ and $X$
is m, so N\,m$^{-3}$. $M_{12}Z$: $\ii k\eta\Gcal(C_{13}+C_{44})$ is m$^{-2}$Pa and $Z$ is m.
$M_{13}W$: $\ii k\eta(R_1+R_3)$ is m$^{-2}$Pa, because the phonon--phason coefficients $R$ are moduli and
carry Pa; and $W$ is m. $M_{14}\Phi$: $\ii k\eta(e_{31}+e_{15})$ is C\,m$^{-4}$ and $\Phi$ is V, so
J\,m$^{-4}$ = N\,m$^{-3}$. $M_{15}\Theta$: $\ii k\beta_1$ is Pa\,m$^{-1}$K$^{-1}$ and $\Theta$ is K. Row 1
is consistent.
\end{worked}

\begin{worked}
Row 3 is the phason balance, and it is the row worth checking most carefully because its dimension is not
the one an elastic habit suggests. The balance reads
$H_{zx,x}+H_{zz,z}=\rho_p\ddot w+\gamma\dot w$, and $H$ is a stress, so the left side is N\,m$^{-3}$. On
the right, $\rho_p\ddot w$ is kg\,m$^{-3}\cdot$m\,s$^{-2}$ = N\,m$^{-3}$, so the phason density $\rho_p$ is
a mass density, kg\,m$^{-3}$. The friction term $\gamma\dot w$ must then be N\,m$^{-3}$, and $\dot w$ is
m\,s$^{-1}$, so $\gamma$ carries N\,s\,m$^{-4}$ = kg\,m$^{-3}$s$^{-1}$: a friction per unit volume per unit
velocity, not a viscosity, because the phason displacement is not a velocity field of the material.
Now the entries. $M_{31}X$: $\ii k\eta(R_1+R_3)$ is Pa\,m$^{-2}$ and $X$ is m.
$M_{33}W$: $K_1\eta^2$ is Pa\,m$^{-2}$, so $K_1$ and $K_2$ are moduli; $\rho_p\omega^2$ is
kg\,m$^{-3}$s$^{-2}$ = Pa\,m$^{-2}$; $\ii\gamma\omega$ is kg\,m$^{-3}$s$^{-1}\cdot$s$^{-1}$ = kg\,m$^{-3}$s$^{-2}$ = Pa\,m$^{-2}$,
since Pa is kg\,m$^{-1}$s$^{-2}$. All three agree, which is the check that the friction has been given the right unit.
$M_{35}\Theta$: $\eta\beta^{w}$ must be Pa\,m$^{-1}$K$^{-1}$ for the product with $\Theta$ to be
N\,m$^{-3}$, so $\beta^{w}$ carries Pa\,K$^{-1}$: it is a stress per unit temperature, exactly like
$\beta_1$ and $\beta_3$, and that is the natural unit for the extension this paper proposes. Row 3 is
consistent.
\end{worked}

\begin{worked}
Row 4 is Gauss' law, C\,m$^{-3}$, and its entries are the same as in the companion paper:
$\ii k\eta(e_{15}+e_{31})$ is C\,m$^{-4}$, $\epsilon_{33}\eta^2$ is C\,V$^{-1}$m$^{-3}$, $\eta p_3$ is
C\,m$^{-3}$K$^{-1}$. Row 5 is an energy balance, J\,m$^{-3}$s$^{-1}$. $M_{51}X$: $fT_0\omega k\beta_1$ is
Pa\,m$^{-1}$s$^{-1}$ and $X$ is m. $M_{53}W$: $-\ii\omega fT_0\beta^{w}\eta$ is
s$^{-1}$K$\,$Pa\,K$^{-1}$m$^{-1}$ = Pa\,m$^{-1}$s$^{-1}$ and $W$ is m. $M_{54}\Phi$:
$\ii\omega fT_0p_3\eta$ is C\,m$^{-3}$s$^{-1}$ and $\Phi$ is V. $M_{55}\Theta$:
$-\ii\omega f\rho c_p-k_{\rm th}L$ is J\,m$^{-3}$K$^{-1}$s$^{-1}$ and $\Theta$ is K. Rows 4 and 5 are
consistent.
\end{worked}

\section{Calculation J: the two family matrices}

\begin{worked}
The split holds here for every setting of the couplings, so the fourteen-by-fourteen can be replaced by two
seven-by-seven, and it is worth writing out why. Take a pair of roots $\pm\eta_m$. The amplitude vectors
are related by the column signs $\tau=(+1,-1,-1,-1,+1)$ found in Section~\ref{sec:calcD}, that is
$\mathbf a(-\eta_m)=\operatorname{diag}(\tau)\mathbf a(\eta_m)$: $\hat u_x$ and $\hat\theta$ keep their
sign, $\hat u_z$, $\hat w$ and $\hat\varphi$ change theirs.
Substitution: form the two combinations
$\mathbf a(\eta_m)e^{\eta_mz}\pm\operatorname{diag}(\tau)\mathbf a(\eta_m)e^{-\eta_mz}$.
Collect component by component: the plus combination gives $2a_j\cosh\eta_mz$ where $\tau_j=+1$ and
$2a_j\sinh\eta_mz$ where $\tau_j=-1$, so $\hat u_x$ and $\hat\theta$ are even in $z$ and $\hat u_z$,
$\hat w$, $\hat\varphi$ are odd. The minus combination does the opposite.
Result: the seven roots with $\operatorname{Re}\eta_m\ge0$, the seven conditions at one face, and a
$7\times7$ matrix per family whose entry is
$B^{\rm sym}_{rm}=\sum_j c_{rj}(\eta_m)a_j(\eta_m)\bigl(e^{\eta_mh}+\tau_je^{-\eta_mh}\bigr)$,
with the $c_{rj}$ of Section on the column of $\mathbf B$; the antisymmetric matrix is the same with
$e^{\eta_mh}-\tau_je^{-\eta_mh}$.
\end{worked}

\begin{worked}
Check the count. Seven roots and seven rows in each family, seven plus seven is fourteen conditions in
all, which is what the fourteen-by-fourteen carries. The two routes must give the same branches to the
tolerance of the root finder, and that agreement is one of the self-tests: it is a check on the amplitude
ratios, on the face rows and on the parity argument at once. If they disagree, the error is in one of those
three places and nowhere else.
\end{worked}

\subsection*{Why each test is a test}

\begin{worked}
The elastic test is the only one with an independent answer: with every coupling off and both lengths zero
the plate is an elastic plate whose dispersion was computed fifty years ago, and any disagreement is ours.
The phason-off test is next: it removes one field and one row and must return a model that appears in the
literature, so a failure there is either a wrong entry in the $R$ terms or a wrong face condition, and
nothing else. The parity test is a test of the algebra rather than of the physics: it is decided by the
parities of the entries, and a root finder that does not show the pairing has not tested anything. The
continuity test in $l_g$ and $l_{\rm nl}$ is a test of the assembly: the ten physical roots must move
continuously as the lengths go to zero and the four extra roots must leave, so a jump is a bookkeeping
error in the boundary conditions. The two-route test, determinant against singular value, is a test of
conditioning: the two locate the same zero by different routes, and if they disagree the matrix is too ill
conditioned for the arithmetic being used rather than wrong.
Result: five tests, each of which fails in a different place, which is what makes them worth running in
that order.
\end{worked}

\begin{worked}
Two more that belong in the paper rather than in the checking. The first is the crossover map: sweep the
frequency through $\omega\sim\gamma/\rho_p$ at fixed wavenumber and record where the phason branch changes
from diffusive to propagating. The prediction is a change of character and not a number, so the test is
qualitative and does not rest on the constants. The second is the size effect: sweep $l_g/h$ and $l_{\rm nl}/h$ at fixed $kh$ and
record the direction of the shift of the lowest symmetric branch. The prediction is that the gradient
length stiffens and the nonlocal length softens, and the two must not be confused, which is easy to do
because both enter as squares.
\end{worked}

\section{Calculation K: why seven conditions and not five}

\begin{worked}
Count the amplitudes first. The degree of $\det M$ is the number of partial waves, because each root
$\eta_m$ contributes one wave and one unknown amplitude $A_m$. With $l_g\ne0$ the count of
Section~\ref{sec:calcD} gives fourteen, with $l_g=0$ it gives ten. Each face supplies half of them, so
seven conditions per face in the first case and five in the second.
Now count the other way, by field, and check that the two agree. There are five fields, so five conditions
per face is one per field: a traction, a shear traction, an electric condition, a carrier or phason
condition and a thermal condition. The two extra conditions in the gradient case are the two double
tractions, and they are extra because the gradient length raises the order of the mechanical equations in
$z$ from two to four: $\Gcal$ carries $\eta^2$ and multiplies the $\eta^2$ that the strains already carry,
so $M_{11}$ and $M_{22}$ are quartic in $\eta$ and the two momentum rows are fourth order in $z$.
Collect: five fields plus two extra orders is seven, and seven per face times two faces is fourteen, which
is the number of amplitudes. The two counts agree, which is the check.
\end{worked}

\begin{worked}
The same count says what happens at the degenerate edges. At $l_g=0$ the double tractions vanish and the
matrix drops to ten, so five conditions per face; keeping the two double-traction rows with $l_g=0$ would
give two rows of zeros and a singular matrix, which is the commonest way to get a spurious zero branch in
this problem. At $\eta=0$ the carrier flux row vanishes identically, because every term in it carries one
$\eta$, so a root at the origin has to be treated on its own rather than through the general assembly.
Result: the count is not a formality; it is where the degenerate cases announce themselves.
\end{worked}

\section{Self-test list}\label{sec:selftest}

\begin{enumerate}
\item All couplings off and both size lengths zero: the classical Rayleigh--Lamb plate, both families.
\item Phason off: the piezoelectric thermoelastic plate of the companion paper, same branch count.
\item $\gamma=0$ with $\rho_p\ne0$: a propagating phason branch; $\rho_p=0$ with $\gamma\ne0$: a diffusive
one; both with the same crossover.
\item The parity statement of Calculation D, checked twice: from the roots (do they pair?) and from the two
smaller family matrices (do their determinants vanish at the same $k$?).
\item $l_g\to0$ and $l_{\rm nl}\to0$ separately, continuously, with the gradient length stiffening and the
nonlocal length softening.
\item The count of rows in $\mathbf B$ against the count of roots, in both settings of $l_g$.
\item Dimensions of rows 1--3 against each other; $R$ and $K$ must enter with the same unit.
\end{enumerate}
