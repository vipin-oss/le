1. Introduction
Modern technology has brought significant advancements in transportation, with numerous railway tracks connecting cities and
an extensive network of roads and bridges facilitating convenient vehicle movement. Researchers have consistently focused on the
vibrations generated by high-speed trains and vehicles by studying the interactions among train-track systems, vehicle-pavement
systems, and vehicle-bridge systems. As a result, one of the primary challenges in engineering is to analyze the dynamic response of
layered geological structures exposed to moving loads. The stress produced by a moving load within a material is an important topic in
mechanics, with wide applications in assessing the stability and reliability of engineering structures. Dynamic investigations of struc-
tures subjected to moving loads have gained significant importance owing to their wide applicability in contemporary transportation
engineering, particularly in the design of high-speed railway systems and the maintenance of highway and transit infrastructure [1].
The theoretical framework describing the response of elastic media under moving loads has progressively developed into a funda-
mental component of modern solid mechanics. Initial analytical formulations focused on evaluating stress fields produced by slowly
translating loads acting on an elastic half-space, with pioneering contributions provided by Sneddon [2]. These early formulations
motivated further extensions in which transient dynamic behavior and boundary-induced effects were incorporated, notably through
the works of Ang [3], Payton [4], and Sackman [5]. As the theory matured, attention shifted toward more realistic physical config-
urations, including the role of surface nonuniformities and geometric imperfections. In this direction, Chatterjee and Chattopadhyay
[6] examined how parabolic irregularities modify stress characteristics in floating ice sheets subjected to moving loads. Contempo-
rary research has expanded the modeling framework even further by considering complex material architectures such as functionally
graded, anisotropic, and porous media, thereby enhancing the applicability of moving-load analysis to advanced engineering and
geophysical systems [7–9].
Viscoelasticity is a fundamental material property that characterizes substances exhibiting both viscous and elastic responses
during deformation. Such behavior naturally arises in geological materials due to the movement and interaction of tectonic plates
under high pressure. The internal layers of the Earth largely consist of viscoelastic materials and also contain fluid-filled regions
that display viscoelastic effects. Meghana et al. [10] investigated shear-wave-induced vibrations in a nonlocal structure composed
of loosely bonded flexo-viscoelastic material. Garg [11] analyzed the response of viscoelasticity on the characteristics of harmonic
waves through a deformable medium. Chattopadhyay et al. [12] further extended the study of wave propagation in layered crustal
structures by incorporating viscoelastic characteristics into their model. Several studies [13–15] have emphasized the important role
of viscosity in accurately describing seismic wave propagation.
Piezoelectric materials are substances that generate an internal electrical charge when subjected to mechanical stress. The term
“piezo” originates from the Greek word meaning “to press.” A variety of naturally occurring materials exhibit the piezoelectric effect,
including DNA, enamel, silk, bone, dentine, and several crystalline or ceramic substances. In surface acoustic wave (SAW) devices,
this effect is employed through interdigitated transducers (IDTs), which convert an electrical signal into a mechanical surface wave
and subsequently reconvert it into an electrical output. Consequently, piezoelectric materials play a central role in SAW devices,
sensors, and transducers used for the generation and propagation of surface waves [16–20]. Chaudhary et al. [21] studied the stresses
induced by a dynamic load on a prestressed piezoelectric substrate. Erturk et al. [22] explored piezoelectric energy harvesting for
civil infrastructure, emphasizing the effects of moving loads and resulting strain variations. Amini et al. [23] studied the harvesting
of piezoelectric energy from beam vibrations excited by multiple moving loads. Despite their wide range of applications, electrome-
chanical devices based on single-phase piezoelectric substrates face inherent limitations due to their high density and large acoustic
impedance, which degrade device performance. These materials are also brittle, making them susceptible to mechanical and electrical
failure.
Several material parameters, including elastic constants, porosity, dielectric properties, density, and viscosity, significantly in-
fluence the piezoelectric response. Among these, porosity is a key tunable feature that can be adjusted to meet specific functional
requirements, such as enhancing sensor sensitivity or reducing acoustic impedance. Introducing porosity reduces material density and
helps minimize impedance mismatch. Porous piezoelectric materials also find applications in vibratory sensors, contact microphones,
and underwater transducers due to their adaptable structural characteristics. When a polymer matrix is incorporated into porous
piezoceramics, the resulting composite displays greater flexibility and simplifies fabrication compared to single-phase systems. The
presence of passive polymers additionally imparts viscoelastic characteristics to the composite [24]. In such materials, the effective
elastic, piezoelectric, and dielectric constants become complex quantities influenced by mechanical and electrical losses and their
coupling through piezoelectric interactions. Kar-Gupta et al. [25] developed a three-dimensional finite element model to investigate
the electromechanical behavior of porous piezoelectric materials with improved accuracy. Chen and co-authors [26] studied the
vibration characteristics of a double functionally graded porous beam subjected to a moving load. Rakshith et al. [27] examined the
stresses generated in a visco-porous piezoelectric half-space with an irregular surface under the action of a moving load.
Recent investigations have highlighted that wave behavior in piezoelectric media is strongly governed by material imperfections,
mechanical constraints, and surrounding environmental conditions. In particular, structural discontinuities and graded material prop-
erties have been shown to alter stress transmission and dispersion characteristics significantly. Gupta et al. [28] examined SH waves
in graded piezoelectric cylinders, highlighting the effect of imperfect interfaces. The contribution of surface-related effects within
piezoelectric layers has also received considerable attention in the work conducted by Seema et al. [29]. Complementing these find-
ings, Liu et al. [30] examined how initial stress conditions affect Love-wave propagation, emphasizing implications for sensing device
performance. Fluid-structure interaction effects were addressed by Cui et al. [31], who studied attenuation phenomena in layered
media surrounded by viscous fluids.

Further developments include damage-detection methodologies based on wave mechanics, where Moharana [32] proposed a con-
tinuous shear-lag approach linking SH-wave frequency variations to structural degradation. Additionally, Kumhar et al. [33] explored
wave dynamics in piezo-composite porous media accounting for interfacial discontinuities, demonstrating their relevance to modern
biosensing applications. Surface flaws and interfacial imperfections can substantially modify stress evolution in multilayer piezoelec-
tric systems. Geometric irregularities likewise play a critical role in acoustic and elastic wave transmission, rendering their inclusion
necessary for precise modeling of piezoelectric plates, smart material systems, and geophysical structures [34,35]. These geometric
non-uniformities frequently induce guided-wave dispersion, attenuation, and mode transformation, whether manifested as surface
corrugations, waviness, or microscale roughness, thereby diminishing sensing and actuation performance [36,37]. Furthermore, elec-
tromechanical interaction, dynamic rigidity, and interfacial stress transfer are strongly influenced by imperfect bonding conditions
in multilayered microstructured media.
The concept of nonlocality, which extends the framework of traditional solid mechanics, describes the behavior of a material
point by accounting for its interactions with other points within a finite spatial domain rather than only its immediate neighborhood.
Nonlocal elasticity generates a strain field that is compatible with the lattice-dynamic behavior at discrete atomic sites. This theory
effectively characterizes nanoscale material behavior by incorporating an intrinsic length-scale parameter. Owing to their extremely
low mass and high sensitivity, nanostructures play a vital role in the development of nanoelectromechanical systems for computing,
biosensing, and biomedical applications. Among various nanostructural materials, carbon nanotubes stand out due to their exceptional
mechanical, thermal, and electrical properties, making them a subject of extensive research.
The foundational principles of nonlocal elasticity were introduced by Eringen and Edelen [38]. Eringen [39] further demonstrated
that nonlocal elasticity predicts a finite stress at the tip of a fracture, in contrast to classical elasticity, which yields a singular (infinite)
stress. Eringen’s theory of nonlocality has been widely applied across multiple domains, including vibration and buckling analyses
of carbon nanotubes [40–42], studies on hygro-thermal vibrations in nanobeams [43,44], and evaluations of natural frequencies in
nanoscale functionally graded rectangular plates [45]. A new study by Zhu et al.[46] provides a framework for the smart vibra-
tion control of curved nanostructures, using piezoelectricity to counteract the complex, size-dependent shaking that occurs at the
nanoscale. The studies of Fang et al.[47–49] have extensively researched the dynamics of nanoscale structures, specifically focusing
on the nonlinear vibrations of layered semiconductor plates and the size-dependent behavior of curved, piezoelectric-coated beams
and also effect of material gradients on the stability of these nanoscale components. Gupta et al. [50] examined the propagation of
SH-waves generated by a line source in a nonlocal viscoelastic medium. Additionally, Dutta et al. [51] investigated the propagation
of SH-waves under impulsive loading in a multilayered system composed of a functionally graded substrate and a nonlocal fiber-
reinforced layer. As a result, nonlocal theory continues to be extensively employed in the analysis of wave propagation across various
engineered and natural media.
The mechanical response of layered media subjected to moving loads has attracted sustained attention due to its relevance in
geomechanics and smart structural systems. Mahanty et al. [7] examined stress localization generated by dynamic loading in a
poroelastic layer supported by a transversely isotropic half-space, providing insight into load-induced deformation mechanisms in
heterogeneous substrates. Building upon this foundation, Kumar et al. [7] investigated the influence of moving loads on stress evo-
lution within a composite configuration consisting of a porous viscoelastic layer coupled with a transversely isotropic viscoelastic
medium. Their findings emphasized the significant role of material damping and porosity in governing stress redistribution.
Subsequent developments incorporated size-dependent effects and structural irregularities into moving-load analysis. Gupta et
al. [50] conducted a nonlocal investigation of stress behavior in an irregular functionally graded fiber-reinforced system imperfectly
attached to a reinforcing substrate, highlighting the importance of interface conditions and nonlocal elasticity in predicting realistic
mechanical responses. Nevertheless, a unified framework accounting simultaneously for porosity, piezoelectric coupling, surface
irregularity, and nonlocal interactions in micro- and nanoscale piezoelectric plates under moving-load excitation remained largely
unexplored. Recently, Meghana et al. [52] analyzed the dynamic response of a nonlocal porous piezoelectric plate caused by a
moving load on the upper irregular surface. Although this work has bridged the aforementioned gap, the behavior of a loosely
bonded viscoelasic/porous piezoelectric bilayer microplate with respect to a moving load was still an important direction for further
investigation. In particular, the application of Eringen’s nonlocal elasticity theory to analyze moving-load-induced dynamics in a
bilayered configuration composed of a nonlocal viscoelastic medium bonded to a nonlocal porous piezoelectric layer with an imperfect
interface has not yet been systematically addressed. Authors considered this bilayered model due to some additional advantages than
the model containing only nonlocal porous piezoelectric microplate. Firstly, Piezoelectric materials are often brittle. A viscoelastic
top layer acts as a buffer distributing the moving load more evenly and reducing localized stress concentrations, particularly those
caused by surface irregularities or friction. Secondly, a structure incorporating a layer with viscoelastic property overlying porous
piezoelectric layer can improve acoustic compatibility which is crucial for underwater transducers. Moreover, the top layer protects
the active piezoelectric material from direct frictional contact with the moving load. This reduces the possibility for surface-initiated
fatigue or failure.
Motivated by these research gaps and additional advantages, the present work develops an analytical model aimed at evaluating
nonlocal stress fields generated by a moving load acting on an irregular viscoelastic layer imperfectly bonded to a porous piezoelec-
tric substrate. Such configurations arise in advanced engineering applications, including MEMS-based sensing devices and aerospace
surface coatings, where structures are frequently exposed to high-speed frictional interactions and thermal loading conditions. Ac-
curate prediction of stress concentration and interfacial degradation is therefore essential for mitigating fatigue and delamination in
micro-scale components.
The proposed formulation incorporates parabolic surface irregularity, imperfect interfacial bonding, and porous piezoelectric
material characteristics to achieve a more realistic representation of practical systems. A perturbation-based analytical scheme is

Within the framework of nonlocal elasticity, the mechanical response at a given material point is influenced not only by its
immediate neighborhood but also by points distributed throughout the surrounding medium. However, this influence weakens with
increasing spatial separation due to the gradual decay of long-range intermolecular interactions. As a result, the nonlocal constitutive
kernels exhibit a distance-dependent attenuation, implying that the contribution from remote points diminishes continuously as the
separation between interacting locations increases. In the limit of very large separation distances, the interaction effect becomes
negligible, which is mathematically expressed as

8. Conclusions
This study develops a rigorous analytical framework to examine the nonlocal stress response of a multilayered system composed
of a nonlocal viscoelastic layer imperfectly bonded to a nonlocal porous piezoelectric layer, subjected to a frictional moving load
over an irregular rough surface. By incorporating viscosity, porosity, imperfect interfacial bonding, geometric irregularities, and
Eringen’s nonlocal elasticity, the proposed model closely represents realistic engineering situations encountered in pavements, rail
track foundations, layered geomaterials, and smart micro electromechanical structures. The graphical analysis provides clear insights
into how the key parameters, namely the frictional coefficient, viscosity, irregularity depth, and irregularity factor, govern the induced
nonlocal normal and shear stresses in both layers. The major observations of the study are summarized below:
• The nonlocal normal and shear stresses in the viscoelastic layer show very weak sensitivity to the frictional coefficient, whereas
the porous piezolayer exhibits a strong friction-dependent amplification, particularly in the shear component.
i. The frictional effect is most intense at the surface contact zone and decays rapidly as depth (𝑧∕𝐻 ) increases. This suggests
0
that the lateral energy injected by the moving load is primarily absorbed by the shallow regions of the microplate through
shear deformation.
ii. The frictional parameter primarily drives horizontal traction, which significantly amplifies the shear stress (𝜏 ) in the porous
𝑧𝑥
piezolayer through direct energy injection at the contact surface. In contrast, the normal stress (𝜏 ) remains largely unaffected
𝑧𝑧
by friction because it is governed by the vertical weight and impact of the moving load, which do not change with surface
roughness.
• Viscosity has a negligible influence on the stress components of the viscoelastic layer but significantly reduces the normal stress
and moderately reduces the shear stress in the porous piezoelectric layer. Viscosity primarily affects shear stress in the shallow
region of the NPPL, while its impact weakens with increasing depth as the shear stress itself becomes very small.
i. The viscoelastic layer’s stress state is primarily dictated by its structural stiffness and the external load rather than its internal
fluid-like damping.
ii. Conversely, in the porous piezoelectric layer, high viscosity acts as an efficient energy sink that cushions the material against
the moving load, significantly dampening the vertical compression (normal stress) and lateral sliding forces (shear stress).
• The irregularity factor (𝑥∕𝑠) causes almost no variation in stresses within the viscoelastic layer, whereas in the porous piezoelectric
layer both normal and shear stresses consistently decrease as 𝑥∕𝑠 increases.
i. The viscoelastic layer acts as a protective buffer, where its inherent damping properties isolate the internal stress distribution
from the geometric perturbations of the parabolic irregularity.
ii. In contrast, the porous piezoelectric layer is highly sensitive to the surface topography (𝑥∕𝑠), where an increase in the irregu-
larity factor effectively diffuses the incoming energy of the moving load, leading to a consistent reduction in both normal and
shear stress magnitudes.
iii. This indicates that while the viscoelastic material maintains structural stability regardless of surface flaws, the piezolayer’s
response can be "tuned" or mitigated by controlling the geometric profile of the microplate.
• As the velocity of the moving load increases, the magnitude of the induced stress response generally decreases. This behavior
arises because, at higher speeds, the load remains in contact with a given point for a shorter duration, preventing the material
from undergoing full deformation or settlement. From a physical standpoint, this phenomenon can be interpreted in terms of
the apparent increase in material stiffness under rapid loading conditions. The material exhibits a more rigid response since its
microscopic constituents, such as atoms and fibers, do not have sufficient time to rearrange or dissipate energy effectively.
• The viscoelastic top layer remains largely insensitive to geometric and frictional variations due to intrinsic damping, while the
porous piezoelectric substrate displays strong sensitivity to material parameters, surface geometry, and nonlocal interactions.
Overall, the study emphasizes that the viscoelastic top layer, due to its damping characteristics, exhibits limited sensitivity to exter-
nal geometric and frictional variations, whereas the porous piezoelectric substrate responds strongly to both material and geometric
parameters. These findings provide useful insights for the optimal design of layered smart structures, vibration-resistant pavements,
rail-track bedding systems, and micro/nano-scale devices where nonlocal effects, porosity, and interface imperfections play a crucial
role.
