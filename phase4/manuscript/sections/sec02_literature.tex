\section{Literature Landscape and Scholarly Context}
\label{sec:literature}

\subsection{Foundational works}

The literature of generalized continuum mechanics for wave propagation rests on a small number of foundational works that defined entire theory families:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Biot (1956) \cite{Biot1956}} — Classical coupled thermoelasticity, combining Fourier heat conduction with linear elasticity. This established the framework for all subsequent generalized theories and identified the infinite propagation speed paradox.
\item \textbf{Cattaneo (1948) \cite{Cattaneo1948} and Vernotte (1958) \cite{Vernotte1958}} — Hyperbolic heat conduction with relaxation time, proposing finite thermal wave speed independently in Italy and France.
\item \textbf{Lord and Shulman (1967) \cite{Lord1967}} — Generalized thermoelasticity with a single thermal relaxation time, making the heat equation hyperbolic and restoring finite thermal wave speed.
\item \textbf{Green and Lindsay (1972) \cite{Green1972}} — Two-temperature thermoelasticity with two relaxation times, including dissipationless propagation for centrally symmetric bodies.
\item \textbf{Green and Naghdi (1991, 1993) \cite{Green1991,Green1993}} — Types II and III theories, introducing thermal displacement and dissipationless thermoelasticity (type II) and dissipative type III.
\item \textbf{Tzou (1995, 1996, 1997) \cite{Tzou1995,Tzou1997}} — Dual-phase-lag and triple-phase-lag models for micro-scale heat transfer with two and three phase lags, bridging macro and micro scales.
\item \textbf{Eringen (1972, 1974, 1983) \cite{Eringen1972,Eringen1974,Eringen1983}} — Nonlocal elasticity where stress at a point depends on strain throughout a neighborhood, enabling scale-dependent modeling of micro- and nanostructures.
\item \textbf{Guyer and Krumhansl (1990) \cite{Guyer1990}} — Second sound in solids, providing experimental and theoretical evidence for thermal wave propagation at low temperatures.
\item \textbf{Joseph and Preziosi (1990) \cite{Joseph1990}} — Comprehensive review of heat waves, establishing the hyperbolic heat conduction framework from kinetic theory to continuum models.
\item \textbf{Wang and Li (2011) \cite{Wang2011}} — Memory-dependent derivatives with integral-form fading memory, an alternative to fractional calculus for history-dependent materials.
\item \textbf{Povstenko (2004, 2009, 2011, 2015) \cite{Povstenko2004,Povstenko2009,Povstenko2011,Povstenko2016}} — Fractional thermoelasticity with time-fractional heat equations for anomalous diffusion, providing a unified mathematical framework for memory effects.
\item \textbf{Caputo (1967) \cite{Caputo1967}} — Linear model of dissipation with almost frequency-independent Q, introducing the fractional derivative that became the foundation of fractional viscoelasticity and thermoelasticity.
\item \textbf{Chandrasekharaiah (1986, 1998) \cite{Chandrasekharaiah1986,Chandrasekharaiah1998}} — Review articles on hyperbolic thermoelasticity and second sound, synthesizing decades of research on generalized theories.
\item \textbf{Shakeriaski et al. (2021) \cite{Shakeriaski2021}} — Recent review of generalized thermoelasticity theory and modified models, surveying the state of the art.
\item \textbf{Dhaliwal and Sherief (1980) \cite{Dhaliwal1980}} — Generalized thermoelasticity for anisotropic media, extending relaxation-time theories to direction-dependent material properties.
\item \textbf{Nowacki (1975) \cite{Nowacki1975}} — Monograph on thermoelasticity providing comprehensive treatment of classical and generalized theories with engineering applications.
\item \textbf{Achenbach (1973) \cite{Achenbach1973}} — Wave propagation in elastic solids, foundational text for elastic wave theory, reflection, and transmission at interfaces.
\end{itemize}

These works form the intellectual backbone of the field. Every generalized theory discussed in this review can be traced to one or more of these foundational references.

\subsection{Contemporary research developments}

Since the foundational period, the field has expanded along several directions:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Coupled multiphysics:} Piezothermoelasticity, magneto-thermo-elasticity, semiconductor photo-thermo-elasticity, and porous/double-porous media have extended the single-field thermoelastic framework to coupled multi-field problems. The micropolar extensions of Eringen (1968a,b) add micro-rotational degrees of freedom for materials with internal structure.
\item \textbf{Memory and heredity:} Memory-dependent derivatives, fractional operators, and hereditary integrals have provided alternatives to classical relaxation-time models, particularly for polymers, biological tissues, and composites. Fractional calculus (Caputo 1967; Gorenflo and Mainardi 1997) provides the mathematical foundation for memory operators.
\item \textbf{Nonlocality and scale effects:} Nonlocal elasticity, strain-gradient theory, and two-phase local/nonlocal models capture size-dependent wave behavior at micro- and nanoscales. Gradient-type formulations (Askes and Aifantis 2006; Polizzotto 2001) and size-effect studies (Maranganti and Sharma 2007; Lakes 1993) provide experimental and theoretical support.
\item \textbf{Interface phenomena:} Wave reflection, transmission, mode conversion, and energy partitioning at interfaces between dissimilar media have been studied for elastic, thermoelastic, piezothermoelastic, porous, and nonlocal media. Imperfect bonding conditions model thin adhesive layers and micro-cracks.
\item \textbf{Applications:} Smart structures, nondestructive evaluation, energy harvesting, geophysics, biomedical ultrasound, and nanodevices have emerged as major application domains.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Existing reviews and the contribution of this article}

Several review articles exist on individual theory families or application domains:

\begin{itemize}
\item Reviews focused on \textbf{nonlocal elasticity} discuss scale-dependent constitutive relations and nanoscale applications (e.g., Lakes 1993; Askes and Aifantis 2006).
\item Reviews on \textbf{fractional thermoelasticity} cover time-fractional heat equations and anomalous diffusion (e.g., Povstenko 2016; Chandrasekharaiah 1998).
\item Reviews on \textbf{piezothermoelasticity} address coupled-field problems in smart materials.
\item Reviews on \textbf{memory-dependent derivatives} survey integral-form memory operators and their applications.
\item Reviews on \textbf{hyperbolic thermoelasticity} synthesize decades of research on relaxation-time theories (e.g., Chandrasekharaiah 1998; Joseph and Preziosi 1990).
\end{itemize}

None of these reviews provides the comprehensive, theory-spanning synthesis offered here. This review's distinctive contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Breadth:} Coverage of all major theory families---classical, LS, GL, GN-II/III, DPL, TPL, MGT, MDD, fractional, nonlocal, piezothermoelastic, porous, micropolar, semiconductor, and magneto-thermo-elastic---in a single coherent framework.
\item \textbf{Mathematical depth:} First-principles derivations of balance laws, constitutive relations, wave equations, dispersion relations, interface conditions, and energy flux, with documented assumptions for each equation.
\item \textbf{Five-axis taxonomy:} An original classification scheme (physical, mathematical, wave, energy, validation dimensions) that enables systematic identification of research gaps and theory relationships.
\item \textbf{Constructive organization:} The review is organized around scientific developments and conceptual themes rather than individual papers, making it readable as a continuous scholarly narrative.
\item \textbf{Original figures and tables:} Original vector figures and synthesis tables illustrate theory evolution, wave types, interface geometry, energy flux channels, nonlocality, memory kernels, theory relationships, and the research taxonomy.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Citation and verification principles}

Per the equation citation policy, equations are selected for detailed exposition based on:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Verification status:} Equations derived from first principles in this manuscript, or drawn from verified primary sources with documented assumptions, are presented as canonical results.
\item \textbf{Validity domain:} Each equation is presented with its assumptions (constitutive, material symmetry, geometry, boundary conditions, thermal theory, frequency convention, normalization, approximation level).
\item \textbf{Open questions:} Equations that cannot be independently verified are identified as open research questions, not presented as definitive results.
\item \textbf{Minimality:} Fewer precise equations are preferred over hundreds of redundant ones (the equation verification register in Appendix A documents all equations and their status).
\end{enumerate}
