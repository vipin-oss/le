\section{Nonlocal and Scale-Dependent Continuum Models}
\label{sec:nonlocal}

This section presents nonlocal and scale-dependent continuum models: Eringen's nonlocal elasticity, strain-gradient theory, size-dependent wave behavior, and their implications for wave propagation at micro- and nanoscales.

\subsection{Nonlocal elasticity (Eringen): integral constitutive relations}
\label{sec:7.1}

Eringen's nonlocal elasticity theory (1972, 1974, 1983) \cite{Eringen1972,Eringen1974,Eringen1983} states that the stress at a point depends on the strain throughout a neighborhood of that point, not just at the point itself:

\begin{equation}
\sigma_{ij}(\bm{x}) = \int_\Omega \alpha(|\bm{x}' - \bm{x}|, \tau)\, \sigma_{ij}^{\mathrm{local}}(\bm{x}')\, d\Omega_{\bm{x}'},
\label{eq:7.1}
\end{equation}

where $\alpha$ is the attenuation (kernel) function and $\tau = e_0 a/\ell$ is the nonlocal parameter with $e_0$ a material constant, $a$ an internal characteristic length (e.g., lattice spacing), and $\ell$ an external characteristic length (e.g., specimen size).

\subsubsection{Differential form}

For special kernel functions (e.g., the Helmholtz kernel), the integral equation reduces to a differential equation:

\begin{equation}
(1 - \xi^2\nabla^2)\sigma_{ij} = \sigma_{ij}^{\mathrm{local}},
\label{eq:7.2}
\end{equation}

where $\xi = e_0 a$ is the nonlocal length scale parameter.

\subsection{Strain-gradient theory: higher-order gradients}
\label{sec:7.2}

Strain-gradient theory extends classical elasticity by including higher-order gradients of strain in the constitutive relations:

\begin{equation}
\sigma_{ij} = C_{ijkl}\varepsilon_{kl} - \ell^2 C_{ijkl}\nabla^2\varepsilon_{kl},
\label{eq:7.3}
\end{equation}

where $\ell$ is a material length scale parameter. This introduces size-dependent effects: the stiffness depends on the gradient of strain, so smaller features exhibit higher apparent stiffness.

\subsection{Size-dependent wave behavior: dispersion from nonlocality}
\label{sec:7.3}

Nonlocality introduces dispersion: wave speed depends on wavelength. The differential form of nonlocal elasticity was developed by Eringen (1983) \cite{Eringen1983}, and gradient-type formulations were studied by Askes and Aifantis (2006) \cite{Askes2006} and Polizzotto (2001) \cite{Polizzotto2001}. Size-dependent elastic effects at the nanoscale were investigated by Maranganti and Sharma (2007) \cite{Maranganti2007} and reviewed by Lakes (1993) \cite{Lakes1993}. For a nonlocal elastic bar, the dispersion relation is:

\begin{equation}
c(k) = \frac{c_0}{\sqrt{1 + \xi^2 k^2}},
\label{eq:7.4}
\end{equation}

where $c_0$ is the classical wave speed, $k$ is the wave number, and $\xi$ is the nonlocal length scale. Long waves ($k \to 0$) approach the classical speed $c_0$; short waves ($k \to \infty$) are strongly slowed. This size-dependent dispersion has been observed in nanowires, nanotubes, and MEMS/NEMS devices.

\subsection{Energy flux in nonlocal media}
\label{sec:7.4}

For nonlocal media, the classical divergence theorem must be modified because the stress at a point depends on strain throughout a neighborhood. The mechanical power $\sigma_{ji}v_i$ at a point includes contributions from the velocity field in a neighborhood, not just at the point.

The energy flux takes the form:

\begin{equation}
J_i = \sigma_{ji}v_i + q_i + J_i^{\mathrm{nonlocal}},
\label{eq:7.5}
\end{equation}

where $J_i^{\mathrm{nonlocal}}$ is the nonlocal correction term arising from the integral constitutive relation.

\subsubsection{Nonlocal correction term}

The nonlocal correction requires deriving the surface integral form of the nonlocal stress power:

\begin{equation}
J_i^{\mathrm{nonlocal}} = \int_\Omega \alpha(|\bm{x}' - \bm{x}|)\, \sigma_{ji}^{\mathrm{local}}(\bm{x}') v_i(\bm{x}')\, d\Omega_{\bm{x}'} - \sigma_{ji}(\bm{x})v_i(\bm{x}).
\label{eq:7.6}
\end{equation}

This derivation requires modifying the divergence theorem for integral-type constitutive relations. The complete nonlocal energy flux remains an open formulation.

\subsection{Interface conditions for nonlocal media}
\label{sec:7.5}

Interface conditions for nonlocal media differ from classical ones because the stress at a point depends on strain in a neighborhood. Additional constitutive boundary conditions may be required at the interface to ensure uniqueness.

For the differential form \eqref{eq:7.2}, the interface conditions include both the classical conditions (displacement and traction continuity) and higher-order conditions arising from the $\nabla^2$ term.
