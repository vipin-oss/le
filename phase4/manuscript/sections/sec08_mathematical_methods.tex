\section{Mathematical Methods for Wave Propagation}
\label{sec:methods}

This section reviews the mathematical methods used for wave propagation analysis in generalized continua: plane wave ansatz, Christoffel equations, secular equations, complex wave numbers, modal normalization, transform methods, and numerical approaches.

\subsection{Plane wave ansatz and harmonic time dependence}
\label{sec:8.1}

For time-harmonic problems, all field variables take the form:

\begin{equation}
\text{field}(\bm{x},t) = \hat{\text{field}}(\bm{x}) e^{-i\omega t},
\label{eq:8.1}
\end{equation}

where $\hat{\text{field}}$ is the complex amplitude and $\omega$ is the angular frequency. The convention $e^{-i\omega t}$ implies that positive imaginary parts of complex wave numbers correspond to spatial attenuation.

\subsection{Christoffel-type equations and wave speeds}
\label{sec:8.2}

Substituting the plane wave ansatz into the governing equations yields the Christoffel-type equation:

\begin{equation}
[\Gamma_{ik}(\bm{n}) - \rho c^2 \delta_{ik}]\hat{u}_k = 0,
\label{eq:8.2}
\end{equation}

where $\Gamma_{ik}(\bm{n})$ is the Christoffel tensor, $\bm{n}$ is the propagation direction, $c$ is the wave speed, and $\hat{u}_k$ is the polarization vector.

Non-trivial solutions require:

\begin{equation}
\det[\Gamma_{ik}(\bm{n}) - \rho c^2 \delta_{ik}] = 0.
\label{eq:8.3}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Secular equations and characteristic polynomials}
\label{sec:8.3}

The characteristic equation from \eqref{eq:8.3} is a polynomial in $c^2$ (or equivalently in the wave number $q$). For coupled thermo-piezoelastic media, this is typically an eighth-order polynomial:

\begin{equation}
G_1 q^8 + G_2 q^6 + G_3 q^4 + G_4 q^2 + G_5 = 0,
\label{eq:8.4}
\end{equation}

where the coefficients $G_i$ depend on material properties, frequency, and incidence angle.

\subsection{Complex wave numbers: propagating, evanescent, attenuating modes}
\label{sec:8.4}

The roots of the secular equation are classified as:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Propagating modes:} real $q$ --- waves that propagate without spatial attenuation,
\item \textbf{Evanescent modes:} purely imaginary $q$ --- waves that decay exponentially with distance,
\item \textbf{Attenuating modes:} complex $q$ --- waves that both propagate and attenuate.
\end{enumerate}

Only outgoing modes satisfying the radiation condition are retained in the physical solution.

\subsection{Modal normalization for energy flux computation}
\label{sec:8.5}

For energy flux computation, each mode must be consistently normalized. For propagating modes:

\begin{equation}
\hat{u}_i^{(n)} \hat{u}_i^{(n)*} = 1,
\label{eq:8.5}
\end{equation}

where $^*$ denotes complex conjugation. For evanescent modes, normalization is chosen so that the time-averaged energy flux is real and positive. Consistent normalization across modes is essential for energy ratio computations.

\subsection{Transform methods: Fourier and Laplace}
\label{sec:8.6}

Fourier transforms are used for steady-state harmonic problems. Fractional calculus methods were developed by Gorenflo and Mainardi (1997) \cite{Gorenflo1997} and Luchko (2010) \cite{Luchko2010}.

\begin{equation}
\hat{f}(\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(t) e^{i\omega t}\,dt.
\label{eq:8.6}
\end{equation}

Laplace transforms are used for transient problems:

\begin{equation}
\tilde{f}(s) = \int_0^{\infty} f(t) e^{-st}\,dt.
\label{eq:8.7}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Spectral methods and numerical approaches}
\label{sec:8.7}

Spectral methods expand the solution in terms of orthogonal basis functions (e.g., Fourier series, Chebyshev polynomials). These are particularly effective for problems with periodic boundary conditions or smooth solutions.

Numerical approaches include finite element, finite difference, and boundary element methods for problems without closed-form solutions. Nonlocal and memory-dependent models require specialized numerical treatments due to their integral-form constitutive relations.

\subsection{Variational structure and its use as an analytical tool}
\label{sec:8.8}

Alongside the direct transform route, coupled thermoelasticity admits a variational formulation, and the two are not simply equivalent. Variational principles for the coupled system have been established and compared by Marotti de Sciarra (2013) \cite{Marotti2013}, who showed that the admissible function spaces and the boundary conditions inherited from each principle differ, and that the resulting weak formulations do not all select the same class of solutions. This matters practically because a variational derivation constrains the admissible boundary conditions before the equations are solved, and a formulation that admits non-physical boundary data can contaminate the solution even when the differential equations are correct. The variational route is also the one that extends most naturally to nonlocal and gradient constitutive laws, since the integral kernel appears as an operator in the quadratic functional rather than as an explicit convolution in the equations of motion.
