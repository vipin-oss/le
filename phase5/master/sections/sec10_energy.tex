\section{Energy Transport and Dissipation}
\label{sec:energy}

Constitutive theories carry energy-storage, transport, and dissipation consequences, but the expressions depend on their state variables and field equations. This section derives the classical local flux under explicit conventions, states the interface balance at a general level, and records family-specific flux claims as open unless their free energy, constitutive evolution, boundary power, and dissipation inequality have been checked. No universal generalized-continuum flux is assumed.

\subsection{General energy balance}
\label{sec:10.1}

For classical thermoelasticity, with specific internal energy $e$, the total energy per unit volume is

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{E} = \frac{1}{2}\rho v_i v_i + \rho e,
\label{eq:10.1}
\end{equation}

and the local balance (with no internal heat source) takes the form

\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial\mathcal{E}}{\partial t} + \partial_i J_i = \rho f_i v_i,
\label{eq:10.2}
\end{equation}

where $\rho f_i v_i$ is mechanical power per unit volume. Combining momentum balance with the first law yields the classical flux in the next subsection. Equation~\eqref{eq:10.2} is not asserted here as a universal generalized-multiphysics identity: additional field energy, external electrical or magnetic power, interface storage, and internal dissipation must be derived for each specified model.

\subsection{Classical energy flux}
\label{sec:10.2}

For classical coupled thermoelasticity, adding the mechanical power and internal-energy balances yields the outward-positive flux

\begin{equation}
J_i = q_i - \sigma_{ij}v_j,
\label{eq:10.3}
\end{equation}
the classical energy flux. Under this outward-positive convention the mechanical contribution is $-\sigma_{ij}v_j$ and the thermal contribution is $q_i$.

For time-harmonic mechanical fields with convention $e^{-i\omega t}$, the outward-positive mean flux is

\begin{equation}
\langle J_i\rangle_t=\bar q_i-\frac12\Re[\hat{\sigma}_{ij}\hat{v}_j^*],
\label{eq:10.4}
\end{equation}

where $\bar q_i$ is the separately evaluated mean heat flux (zero for a purely oscillatory perturbation about a uniform reference state). The factor $1/2$ and complex conjugate follow from time averaging the real fields. For an interface problem the quantity of interest is the normal component $J_n = J_i n_i$; the sign convention and the normal direction must be stated consistently.

A point worth keeping in view is that the classical flux contains \emph{no} term associated with the internal variables that generalized theories introduce. The entire difficulty of Section~\ref{sec:10.4} follows from that absence.





\subsection{Interface energy balance}
\label{sec:10.3}

Consider an interface $\Sigma$ between two media with unit normal $\bm{n}$. Integrating the general energy balance over a thin pillbox straddling the interface and taking the thinness limit gives the jump condition

\begin{equation}
\jump{J_n} = \mathcal{P}_\Sigma - \frac{\partial\mathcal{E}_\Sigma}{\partial t},
\label{eq:10.5}
\end{equation}
where $J_n = J_i n_i$ is the normal energy flux, $\mathcal{P}_\Sigma$ is the mechanical power input at the interface, and $\mathcal{E}_\Sigma$ is the interfacial energy density.

For a passive interface with no mechanical power input and no interfacial energy storage,

\begin{equation}
\jump{J_n} = 0,
\label{eq:10.6}
\end{equation}
that is, the normal energy flux is continuous across the interface. Two qualifications are needed before this condition is used numerically. First, it is a statement about the \emph{correct} energy flux of the theory in which it is used; a flux built from the classical form alone, applied to a family with internal variables, will not satisfy it even when the mathematics is otherwise flawless. Second, the continuity of the normal flux does not by itself imply the scalar conservation identity of Section~\ref{sec:10.5}: that identity is a statement about modal energies, while \eqref{eq:10.6} is a statement about a single component of a flux at a single surface, and the two are related only after the modal decomposition and normalisation conventions have been fixed.

\subsection{Energy transport in generalized theories: the internal-variable structure}
\label{sec:10.4}

The first-law balance retains the boundary power terms for a specified constitutive theory. What changes between generalized theories is the stored free energy and dissipation, which follow from that theory's state variables and evolution laws. There is no universal additional flux term $-\sum_\alpha Y^\alpha\dot z^\alpha$: this has units of power per volume, not energy flux, unless an additional spatial transport structure is specified. A family-specific identity therefore requires the model's free energy, evolution law, and boundary terms.

\subsection{Conservation identity: \texorpdfstring{$\sum R + \sum T + A_{\mathrm{int}} = 1$}{R + T + A int = 1}}
\label{sec:10.5}

For a specified interface model, complete modal energy accounting with a common normalization can be written as a total-field balance among the reflected, transmitted, and interface-residual terms:

\begin{equation}
\sum R + \sum T + A_{\mathrm{int}} = 1,
\label{eq:10.11}
\end{equation}

where $A_{\mathrm{int}}=(J_{1,n}^{\mathrm{tot}}-J_{2,n}^{\mathrm{tot}})/J_{\mathrm{inc},n}$ is the interface-local residual under the total-field convention of Section~\ref{sec:9.7}; the incident-side and transmitted-side fluxes include all modal cross terms. Bulk dissipation in either half-space is a separate volume contribution and is not added to $A_{\mathrm{int}}$. For a lossless interface with no storage ($A_{\mathrm{int}}=0$),

\begin{equation}
\sum R + \sum T = 1.
\label{eq:10.12}
\end{equation}

Kumar and Kansal report a model-specific interface energy-ratio balance with an interaction term in Eq. (80), supporting inclusion of cross terms rather than a universal identity \cite{Kumar2017}. Kumar et al. define ratios using average energy flux per incident-wave type; those definitions specify normalization but do not establish closure \cite{Kumar2024}. A related study describes its eight-model sum as approximately unity for specified imperfect-interface cases; this is model-specific evidence, not an identity \cite{Kumar2024a}.

Equation~\eqref{eq:10.11} follows algebraically from the total-field definitions; that closure alone is not an independent physical conservation test. Its interpretation as a balance requires the theory-specific flux, complete power channels, the interface storage/power convention, and common definitions of the reported ratios. The lossless reduction additionally requires zero interface-local residual and no net interfacial storage. These conditions are not automatic. In the one-dimensional welded example below, the incident--reflected cross term cancels in the time-averaged normal flux; no zero-flux rule for general coupled or evanescent modes is inferred. Report whether each coefficient is an amplitude or a power ratio and state the flux weighting used.

\subsubsection{Separate interface residual from bulk absorption}

Consider a steady time-averaged one-dimensional control volume with positive normal power-flux magnitudes $P_i$, $P_r$, and $P_t$, all per unit area, at remote incident, reflected, and transmitted reference planes. Let $J_L$ and $J_R$ be the net total-field fluxes on the left and right faces of the interface, both oriented in the positive $x$ direction. For passive, source-free regions with no bulk or interfacial mean energy accumulation, the bulk and interfacial dissipated powers per unit area are

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{D}_L=P_i-P_r-J_L,\qquad
\mathcal{D}_{\Sigma}=J_L-J_R,\qquad
\mathcal{D}_R=J_R-P_t.
\label{eq:10.13}
\end{equation}

Summing the three control-volume balances gives

\begin{equation}
P_i=P_r+P_t+\mathcal{D}_L+\mathcal{D}_{\Sigma}+\mathcal{D}_R.
\label{eq:10.14}
\end{equation}

Thus, with $R=P_r/P_i$ and $T=P_t/P_i$, the remote-plane residual $1-R-T$ already contains both bulk dissipation and local interface dissipation. By contrast, $A_{\mathrm{int}}=\mathcal{D}_{\Sigma}/P_i=(J_L-J_R)/P_i$ is local to the interface. Adding the bulk terms to $1-R-T$ a second time double-counts them; the split in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:10.13}--\eqref{eq:10.14} is checked symbolically in the accompanying verification script. The derivation assumes steady averaged powers, no storage in the bulk control volumes, and no unlisted energy channel; other theories require their complete energy flux and power terms.

\subsubsection{Worked one-dimensional welded-interface system}

For a normal-incidence longitudinal wave at $x=0$ in two lossless one-dimensional bars, let $c_m$ be each bar's longitudinal wave speed and take real positive impedances $Z_m=\rho_m c_m$ and wave numbers $k_m=\omega/c_m$. Under the $e^{-i\omega t}$ convention, the displacement amplitudes are

\begin{equation}
\hat u_1=A_i e^{ik_1x}+A_r e^{-ik_1x},\qquad
\hat u_2=A_t e^{ik_2x}.
\label{eq:10.15}
\end{equation}

Displacement and traction continuity, using $\hat\sigma_{xx}=\rho_m c_m^2\partial_x\hat u_m$, give $A_i+A_r=A_t$ and $Z_1(A_i-A_r)=Z_2A_t$. With $A_r$ and $A_t$ as the two unknown amplitudes, these are two scalar conditions and yield the $2\times2$ system

\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\-Z_1&-Z_2\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}A_r\\A_t\end{bmatrix}
=-A_i\begin{bmatrix}1\\Z_1\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:10.16}
\end{equation}

Its determinant is $-(Z_1+Z_2)$, so the stated positive-impedance case has a unique solution, with amplitude ratios $r=A_r/A_i=(Z_1-Z_2)/(Z_1+Z_2)$ and $t=A_t/A_i=2Z_1/(Z_1+Z_2)$. For the mechanical flux $\langle J_x\rangle=-\tfrac12\Re[\hat\sigma_{xx}\hat v_x^*]$, the incident and reflected power ratios are $R=|r|^2$ and $T=(Z_2/Z_1)|t|^2$. Direct substitution gives $R+T=1$ for this welded, lossless, non-attenuating example; this result is not asserted for an imperfect, dissipative, or generalized interface. The matrix dimensions, determinant, amplitudes, flux weighting, and partition identity are reproduced by \texttt{phase5/verification/verify\_interface\_energy.py}.

\subsection{Dissipation mechanisms}
\label{sec:10.6}

Dissipative mechanisms can include heat-flux relaxation, viscous stress, dielectric loss, interfacial friction, or hereditary memory. Their presence and non-negative dissipation conditions depend on the precise constitutive equations and admissible state space. A single scalar dissipation function or common parameter-ordering rule does not apply uniformly across these families. In particular, the condition $\tau_q\geq\tau_T\geq0$ and a positive Lord--Shulman relaxation time are not, by themselves, a full proof of thermodynamic admissibility or stability. Deriving model-specific free energies and dissipation inequalities remains open.

\subsection{Thermodynamic admissibility: well-posedness, causality, positivity}
\label{sec:10.7}

Thermodynamic admissibility, mathematical well-posedness, and finite-speed propagation are distinct questions. A constitutive theory requires its own free-energy/entropy statement and dissipation inequality; an initial-boundary-value problem requires a separate existence, uniqueness, and continuous-dependence analysis; propagation speed is a property of the selected equations and parameter regime. Fourier heat conduction gives a parabolic equation with instantaneous mathematical support. Statements for relaxation, phase-lag, memory, fractional, and gradient models depend on their exact constitutive laws.

\item Is there a common formal structure under which the energy fluxes of the Lord--Shulman, phase-lag, Moore--Gibson--Thompson, and memory-dependent families become instances of a single template, so that a single derivation suffices for all of them? \label{oq:10.2}
\item Which observable would distinguish the energy partition predicted by two competing theories at the same interface, so that the difference could be measured rather than argued? \label{oq:10.3}
\item How should the entropy production of the conservative Green--Naghdi type~II structure be related to the entropy production of the dissipative branches, given that the former has no dissipation mechanism? \label{oq:10.4}
\item What normalisation of complex modes makes the energy ratios of Eq.~\eqref{eq:10.11} reproducible across independent implementations? \label{oq:10.5}
\item Can a dissipation potential be constructed for the memory-dependent kernel in the spirit of the classical one for the relaxation terms, and does its positivity restrict the admissible kernels to a smaller set than is currently assumed? \label{oq:10.6}
\item What is the relative importance of interface dissipation and bulk dissipation in the absorption coefficient of Eq.~\eqref{eq:10.11} for realistic bonded joints? \label{oq:10.7}
\end{enumerate}
