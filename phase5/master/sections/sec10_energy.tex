\section{Energy Transport and Dissipation}
\label{sec:energy}

Constitutive theories carry energy-storage, transport, and dissipation consequences, but the expressions depend on their state variables and field equations. This section derives the classical local flux under explicit conventions, states the interface balance at a general level, and records family-specific flux claims as open unless their free energy, constitutive evolution, boundary power, and dissipation inequality have been checked. No universal generalized-continuum flux is assumed.

\subsection{General energy balance}
\label{sec:10.1}

For classical thermoelasticity, with specific internal energy $e$, the total energy per unit volume is

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{E} = \frac{1}{2}\rho v_i v_i + \rho e,
\label{eq:10.1}
\end{equation}

and the local balance (with no internal heat source) takes the form

\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial\mathcal{E}}{\partial t} + \partial_i J_i = \rho f_i v_i,
\label{eq:10.2}
\end{equation}

where $\rho f_i v_i$ is mechanical power per unit volume. Combining momentum balance with the first law yields the classical flux in the next subsection. Equation~\eqref{eq:10.2} is not asserted here as a universal generalized-multiphysics identity: additional field energy, external electrical or magnetic power, interface storage, and internal dissipation must be derived for each specified model.

\subsection{Classical energy flux}
\label{sec:10.2}

For classical coupled thermoelasticity, adding the mechanical power and internal-energy balances yields the outward-positive flux

\begin{equation}
J_i = q_i - \sigma_{ij}v_j,
\label{eq:10.3}
\end{equation}
the classical energy flux. Under this outward-positive convention the mechanical contribution is $-\sigma_{ij}v_j$ and the thermal contribution is $q_i$.

For time-harmonic mechanical fields with convention $e^{-i\omega t}$, the outward-positive mean flux is

\begin{equation}
\langle J_i\rangle_t=\bar q_i-\frac12\Re[\hat{\sigma}_{ij}\hat{v}_j^*],
\label{eq:10.4}
\end{equation}

where $\bar q_i$ is the separately evaluated mean heat flux (zero for a purely oscillatory perturbation about a uniform reference state). The factor $1/2$ and complex conjugate follow from time averaging the real fields. For an interface problem the quantity of interest is the normal component $J_n = J_i n_i$; the sign convention and the normal direction must be stated consistently.

A point worth keeping in view is that the classical flux contains \emph{no} term associated with the internal variables that generalized theories introduce. The entire difficulty of Section~\ref{sec:10.4} follows from that absence.

\subsection{Interface energy balance}
\label{sec:10.3}

Consider an interface $\Sigma$ between two media with unit normal $\bm{n}$. Integrating the general energy balance over a thin pillbox straddling the interface and taking the thinness limit gives the jump condition

\begin{equation}
\jump{J_n} = \mathcal{P}_\Sigma - \frac{\partial\mathcal{E}_\Sigma}{\partial t},
\label{eq:10.5}
\end{equation}
where $J_n = J_i n_i$ is the normal energy flux, $\mathcal{P}_\Sigma$ is the mechanical power input at the interface, and $\mathcal{E}_\Sigma$ is the interfacial energy density.

For a passive interface with no mechanical power input and no interfacial energy storage,

\begin{equation}
\jump{J_n} = 0,
\label{eq:10.6}
\end{equation}
that is, the normal energy flux is continuous across the interface. Two qualifications are needed before this condition is used numerically. First, it is a statement about the \emph{correct} energy flux of the theory in which it is used; a flux built from the classical form alone, applied to a family with internal variables, will not satisfy it even when the mathematics is otherwise flawless. Second, the continuity of the normal flux does not by itself imply the scalar conservation identity of Section~\ref{sec:10.5}: that identity is a statement about modal energies, while \eqref{eq:10.6} is a statement about a single component of a flux at a single surface, and the two are related only after the modal decomposition and normalisation conventions have been fixed.

\subsection{Energy transport in generalized theories: the internal-variable structure}
\label{sec:10.4}

The first-law balance retains the boundary power terms for a specified constitutive theory. What changes between generalized theories is the stored free energy and dissipation, which must be derived from that theory's state variables and evolution laws. There is no universal additional flux term $-\sum_\alpha Y^\alpha\dot z^\alpha$: this has units of power per volume, not energy flux, unless an additional spatial transport structure is derived. We therefore do not use a generic internal-variable flux formula; family-specific identities are supplied only when the free energy, evolution law and boundary terms have been established, otherwise the result is marked conditional or open.

\subsubsection{Verified piezothermoelastic energy flux}

A piezothermoelastic flux expression and associated interface-energy ratios are reported in the cited literature. In this revision their signs, harmonic averaging, constitutive definitions, and boundary conditions have not been checked against the cited full texts. The formula and the claim that independent publications confirm it are therefore withheld from the verified set. The mechanical, electrical, and thermal channels must be derived from one stated first-law convention and the coupled constitutive equations before any mode-wise ratio is interpreted. Status: OPEN pending full-text and derivation audit.

\subsubsection{What remains open in the pure generalized theories}

This revision has not derived or full-text-verified generalized-family energy-flux and dissipation identities for Lord--Shulman, Green--Lindsay, Green--Naghdi, phase-lag, Moore--Gibson--Thompson, memory-dependent, or nonlocal formulations. This is a limit of the present audit, not a claim that the wider literature has no such derivations. No general energy-flux identity is asserted here. Each family-specific balance remains OPEN in this version until its free energy, flux variables, boundary powers, dissipation inequality, and sources have been checked.

\subsection{Conservation identity: \texorpdfstring{$\sum R + \sum T + A_{\mathrm{int}} = 1$}{R + T + A int = 1}}
\label{sec:10.5}

For a specified interface model, complete modal energy accounting with a common normalization can be written as a total-field balance among the reflected, transmitted, and interface-residual terms:

\begin{equation}
\sum R + \sum T + A_{\mathrm{int}} = 1,
\label{eq:10.11}
\end{equation}

where $A_{\mathrm{int}}=(J_{1,n}^{\mathrm{tot}}-J_{2,n}^{\mathrm{tot}})/J_{\mathrm{inc},n}$ is the interface-local residual under the total-field convention of Section~\ref{sec:9.7}; the incident-side and transmitted-side fluxes include all modal cross terms. Bulk dissipation in either half-space is a separate volume contribution and is not added to $A_{\mathrm{int}}$. For a lossless interface with no storage ($A_{\mathrm{int}}=0$),

\begin{equation}
\sum R + \sum T = 1.
\label{eq:10.12}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{R3c source-specific energy-partition evidence.} Limited primary sources are available for the exact lossless reduction; GAP-09 remains open. Kumar and Kansal report a model-specific interface energy-ratio balance with an explicit interaction term in Eq. (80); it supports retaining cross terms above, not a universal identity (CONDITIONAL) \cite{Kumar2017}. The eligible own-group source defines ratios from average energy flux per incident-wave type, specifying normalization rather than closure (PRIMARY-SOURCE-CHECKED) \cite{Kumar2024}. Its definitions are not treated as a closure proof; any numerical near-unity result is described only as approximate for its stated model. A related study describes its eight-model sum as close to unity for specified imperfect-interface cases; this is approximate, model-specific evidence, not an identity (CONDITIONAL) \cite{Kumar2024a}.

This identity is conditional on the theory-specific flux, a passive interface, complete total-field modal accounting (including cross terms), and a common normalization. The lossless reduction additionally requires zero interface-local residual and no interfacial storage. These conditions are not automatic. For a single lossless, purely evanescent partial wave, the time-averaged normal flux is zero under the usual real-coefficient assumptions. Cross terms between partial waves can contribute to the total field flux; attenuating modes in dissipative media require separate treatment. Modal accounting must be performed on the complete field. The fourth is the most frequently overlooked: the notion of the energy of a complex mode depends on the normalisation adopted for the modal amplitude, and two groups using different normalisations can report energy ratios that differ by a constant factor while describing the same physics.

\subsection{Dissipation mechanisms}
\label{sec:10.6}

Dissipative mechanisms can include heat-flux relaxation, viscous stress, dielectric loss, interfacial friction, or hereditary memory. Their presence and non-negative dissipation conditions depend on the precise constitutive equations and admissible state space. This version does not assign a universal scalar dissipation function or a common parameter-ordering rule across the families. In particular, the condition $\tau_q\geq\tau_T\geq0$ and a positive Lord--Shulman relaxation time are not, by themselves, a full proof of thermodynamic admissibility or stability. Deriving model-specific free energies and dissipation inequalities remains open.

\subsection{Thermodynamic admissibility: well-posedness, causality, positivity}
\label{sec:10.7}

Thermodynamic admissibility, mathematical well-posedness, and finite-speed propagation are distinct questions. A constitutive theory requires its own free-energy/entropy statement and dissipation inequality; an initial-boundary-value problem requires a separate existence, uniqueness and continuous-dependence analysis; and propagation speed is a property of the selected equations and parameter regime. None of these questions is settled for an entire named model family solely by the family label. Fourier heat conduction gives a parabolic equation with instantaneous mathematical support. The corresponding statements for relaxation, phase-lag, memory, fractional and gradient models require the exact constitutive law and its admissibility conditions. This review does not supply a family-wide proof of well-posedness, causality, or positivity; these remain model-specific audit tasks.

\subsection{Open questions for Section 10}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{OQ10.\arabic*}]
\item What is the explicit time-domain energy flux and dissipation function for each of the families listed in Section~\ref{sec:10.4}, derived from that family's free energy, evolution laws, and boundary-power terms? \label{oq:10.1}
\item Is there a common formal structure under which the energy fluxes of the Lord--Shulman, phase-lag, Moore--Gibson--Thompson, and memory-dependent families become instances of a single template, so that a single derivation suffices for all of them? \label{oq:10.2}
\item Which observable would distinguish the energy partition predicted by two competing theories at the same interface, so that the difference could be measured rather than argued? \label{oq:10.3}
\item How should the entropy production of the conservative Green--Naghdi type~II structure be related to the entropy production of the dissipative branches, given that the former has no dissipation mechanism? \label{oq:10.4}
\item What normalisation of complex modes makes the energy ratios of Eq.~\eqref{eq:10.11} reproducible across independent implementations? \label{oq:10.5}
\item Can a dissipation potential be constructed for the memory-dependent kernel in the spirit of the classical one for the relaxation terms, and does its positivity restrict the admissible kernels to a smaller set than is currently assumed? \label{oq:10.6}
\item What is the relative importance of interface dissipation and bulk dissipation in the absorption coefficient of Eq.~\eqref{eq:10.11} for realistic bonded joints? \label{oq:10.7}
\end{enumerate}
