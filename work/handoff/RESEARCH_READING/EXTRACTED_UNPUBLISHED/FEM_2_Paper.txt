Abstract

This paper develops a displacement-based finite element framework for Mindlin Form-II strain-
gradient elasticity and uses it to study anisotropic size-dependent behavior. The formulation
combines a C1-continuous Bogner–Fox–Schmit (BFS) element with an orientation-dependent
characteristic-length tensor derived from a three-dimensional ellipsoidal microstructure, and
captures the combined effect of microstructural aspect ratio and orientation under simple
shear.  Accuracy is checked against a closed-form analytical benchmark.  Parametric analy-
ses over aspect ratio and orientation angle then examine the displacement field, Cauchy stress,
higher-order double stress, boundary-layer thickness, and strain-gradient energy. Both param-
eters strongly affect the non-classical response: higher-order stresses rise and boundary layers
thicken with increasing anisotropy.  Three-dimensional response surfaces, design maps, and
energy-partition diagrams trace the transition from classical elasticity to gradient-dominated
behavior and show mechanical energy shifting from the classical strain field into the higher-order
gradient term. The framework is suited to architected and cellular materials with anisotropic
microstructures, and to size-dependent analysis of composites, lattice materials, and MEMS
components.
Keywords: Strain-gradient elasticity; Mindlin Form-II theory; Bogner–Fox–Schmit finite el-
ement; Anisotropic microstructure; Boundary-layer mechanics; Gradient energy; Size effect;
Architected materials.


1. Introduction

  Microstructured materials, architected metamaterials, and micro-electro-mechanical systems
(MEMS) appear throughout modern structural engineering.  Classical continuum mechanics,

--- PAGE 2 ---
however, struggles to describe their behavior at small scales. Cauchy elasticity assumes that
stress at a point depends only on the local strain at that same point, an assumption that carries
no intrinsic length scale. The governing equations are therefore scale-invariant [1].
  At the macroscopic scale this simplification works well, because the structural dimensions
are much larger than the material microstructure. At the micro- and nano-scales it breaks
down. Experiments show that smaller components get stiffer as their size approaches the mi-
crostructural length scale, the so-called “smaller is stiffer” effect [2–4]. In printed composites,
micro-cantilevers, and thin adhesive layers, steep deformation gradients appear near interfaces
[5, 6]. Classical Cauchy elasticity cannot distinguish uniform straining from these sharp gra-
dients.  It therefore underpredicts stiffness and misses the boundary layers that form near
constrained surfaces. Higher-order continuum theories address these limitations by accounting
for non-local microstructural interactions [7, 8].
  Several such theories have been developed.  Toupin [9], Mindlin [10, 11], and Koiter [12]
introduced couple stresses and strain gradients into the internal potential energy. Green and
Rivlin [13], Eringen and Suhubi [14], and Capriz [15] proposed complementary generalized
continuum frameworks. Germain’s method of virtual power [16] later gave a systematic way to
derive balance laws involving higher-order stresses and edge interactions [17, 18].
  Mindlin’s Form-II strain-gradient elasticity is a general framework in which the strain-energy
density splits into two parts: a classical strain-energy term and a higher-order gradient-energy
term. The state depends on both the strain tensor and the strain-gradient tensor, and the
response is governed by the Lamé constants and five independent microstructural length-scale
parameters [19–21]. Later work simplified and extended these ideas. Aifantis and co-workers
[22] developed gradient elasticity models that remove stress singularities near cracks and dis-
locations.  Fleck, Hutchinson, and co-workers [23–25] and Gurtin, Anand, and Gudmundson
[26–28] reformulated strain-gradient plasticity to capture size-dependent strengthening in micro-
indentation, torsion, and bending of metals. More recently, additive manufacturing and archi-
tected metamaterials have renewed interest in higher-order elasticity [29–31].  Abali, Müller,
and dell’Isola [32], Auffray et al.  [33], and Shekarchizadeh et al.  [34] have established varia-
tional principles and exact benchmark solutions for Form-II strain-gradient elasticity, showing
that higher-order boundary-layer stiffening comes from the thermodynamic balance between
Cauchy stresses and hyper-stresses.
  Implementing strain-gradient elasticity in the finite element method is demanding [35, 36].
The strain-gradient tensor leads to fourth-order equilibrium equations, so a conforming dis-
cretization requires C1-continuity of the displacement field across element boundaries [37, 38].
Standard C0 Lagrangian elements do not satisfy this requirement; their second derivatives are
discontinuous at interfaces, which causes locking or strong mesh dependence [39].
 Two main strategies address this problem. Mixed finite element methods treat displacements,
displacement gradients, and Lagrange multipliers as independent C0 variables. They avoid C1
continuity but increase the global system size, often to ten or more degrees of freedom per
node, and create saddle-point conditioning problems. Isogeometric analysis based on NURBS
or B-splines achieves the required smoothness over complex domains, but it is computationally
intensive and needs specialized integration schemes [40–43]. An alternative is the Bogner–Fox–
Schmit (BFS) bicubic rectangular element. It keeps displacements, first derivatives, and mixed
second derivatives as nodal degrees of freedom [44, 45], which gives exact inter-element slope
continuity within a compact matrix formulation.  It is an efficient C1-conforming choice for
fourth-order gradient elasticity problems.
  Most numerical studies of strain-gradient elasticity have assumed isotropic characteristic-
length tensors.  In Eringen’s differential nonlocal elasticity, the characteristic-length tensor
comes from volume averaging over a centrosymmetric microstructural neighborhood.  For a

spherical domain it reduces to a diagonal tensor with a single intrinsic length scale [46–48].
Real architected solids, 3D-printed composites, and biological tissues, however, have anisotropic
morphologies with different directional aspect ratios and orientations [49]. Yildizdag and co-
workers [50] recently examined planar flexure and rigid bar pull-out in gradient elasticity and
showed that microstructural orientation strongly affects localized stress transfer. No direct C1-
conforming finite element study has yet examined how microstructural aspect ratio and in-plane
orientation together shape the macroscopic deformation field. In particular, how anisotropic
ellipsoidal microstructures drive the displacement profile, Cauchy-stress redistribution, double-
stress localization, boundary-layer penetration, and the split of energy between classical and
gradient terms is not well characterized.
 The present study develops a C1-conforming finite element framework for anisotropic size-
dependent solids under simple shear. We derive an orientation-dependent characteristic-length
tensor from a general three-dimensional ellipsoidal averaging domain and build it into the com-
plete Mindlin Form-II strain-gradient elasticity formulation. The resulting fourth-order bound-
ary value problem is discretized with the Bogner–Fox–Schmit element, keeping displacements,
displacement gradients, and mixed second derivatives as nodal degrees of freedom. The for-
mulation therefore evaluates exact higher-order strain gradients without mixed-variable spaces,
penalty parameters, or external stabilization. Higher-order Dirichlet boundary conditions and
lateral periodic constraints are enforced through an exact symmetric row-column elimination
scheme that preserves global matrix symmetry and keeps the linear systems well-conditioned.
 The paper first verifies the BFS implementation against the closed-form analytical bench-
mark of Shekarchizadeh et al.  [34] for Case 1 displacement-controlled simple shear, with L2
error norms below 10−7. We then run a parametric study over microstructural orientation an-
gles and aspect ratios. Rotating an elongated ellipsoidal microstructure toward the boundary
normal strengthens non-local interactions: classical Cauchy shear stress is redistributed away
from the upper constraint, and steep compensating higher-order double-stress gradients appear
near the clamped boundaries. We collect these results into three-dimensional orientation–
aspect-ratio maps and boundary-layer thickness contours, which mark where microstructural
boundary layers take over the macroscopic response. An exact thermodynamic energy-partition
analysis using Gauss-Legendre stiffness kernels shows that strongly anisotropic microstructures
can store up to 40% of the input work in higher-order micro-curvature fields. The results map
how microstructural orientation and aspect ratio set the size-dependent response and where
interfacial stress concentration peaks, for architected and cellular materials and for MEMS
devices.

4. Bogner–Fox–Schmit finite element formulation

displacement-based discretization therefore requires C1 continuity. The present work uses the
Bogner–Fox–Schmit (BFS) rectangular element, which is based on tensor-product cubic Hermite
interpolation and provides the required inter-element slope continuity.

parent coordinates (ξ, η) ∈[−1, 1]2 by

5. Numerical verification and simulation setup

 The numerical study has two parts.  First, the BFS implementation is checked against the
closed-form strain-gradient elasticity benchmark of Shekarchizadeh et al. [34], using that bench-
mark’s geometry, isotropic constants, full Mindlin Form-II higher-order matrix DM, and bound-
ary conditions.  Second, the verified implementation is applied to the anisotropic ellipsoidal
problem proposed here, which uses the separable ellipsoidal matrix Dell defined in Eq. (12)
with simple-shear and lateral-periodicity constraints. The two boundary-value problems differ
in both constitutive data and boundary specification, so the benchmark results do not carry
over to the anisotropic simulations.
  All stress, strain, and energy quantities are evaluated directly from the solved finite element
displacement vector using the exact C1-continuous BFS shape-function derivatives. No finite-
difference estimates, curve fitting, data smoothing, or interpolation replacements enter the
post-processing; the Gauss–Legendre quadrature used in the global stiffness assembly is reused
unchanged for every derived field.

5.4. Orientation-dependent engineering shear-strain response
  Taking the first spatial derivative of the displacement field gives the engineering shear strain
γxy = u,y +v,x, evaluated directly from the C1 BFS interpolation. Being a derivative, it is more
sensitive than the displacement to how the length tensor redistributes deformation through the
thickness. Figure 3 shows γxy/γ0, with γ0 = Utop/H, for the same parameters as Fig. 2.
  γxy/γ0 is non-uniform through the thickness, concentrated near the lower boundary and ta-
pering toward the upper one. Both the magnitude and the shape of the profile shift with α.
This is the derivative view of the curvature already seen in the displacement profiles: the same
g222-driven redistribution, now expressed as a strain gradient rather than a displacement curva-
ture. The smoothness of the profiles comes from the C1 interpolation, which gives continuous
first derivatives across element boundaries.

5.5. Orientation-dependent Cauchy shear-stress response
 The Cauchy shear stress follows from the constitutive relation σxy = µ(u,y + v,x), again
evaluated from the exact BFS derivatives. The field is normalized by the classical homogeneous
shear stress µγ0, so a material without microstructural effects would give unity across the height.
 The stress redistributes across the height: it drops near the upper boundary (y/H = 1) and
piles up near the lower one (y/H = 0). From 0◦to 90◦the peak rises monotonically, always near
the lower boundary. Because σxy = µγxy, this is a direct readout of the strain redistribution in
Fig. 3; the underlying cause is the same g222 growth. Changing only the orientation thus shifts
the internal stress distribution and its peak while E and ν stay fixed.
  But the Cauchy stress is only the lower-order part of the force balance in a strain-gradient
continuum. The compensating higher-order forces are next.

5.6. Orientation-dependent higher-order double stress response
 The double stress tensor τ  is the thermodynamic dual to the strain-gradient tensor ∇ε.
Under simple shear the relevant mode is the transverse gradient of the engineering shear strain,
γxy,y = u,yy + v,xy, conjugate to the double shear stress τxyy. It is evaluated from the exact C1
BFS second derivatives:  τxyy = D66 γxy,y = g222(α) µ (u,yy + v,xy). The field is normalized by
µγ0a2.
  τxyy  is localized at both boundaries and decays rapidly to zero in the core.  This is what
balances the strong-form equilibrium along the mid-span, σxy,y −τxyy,yy = 0: wherever the
Cauchy stress has a steep gradient near a constraint, the double stress develops a compensating
one. From 0◦to 90◦the boundary peaks grow, again tracking g222, and the boundary layer
stiffens.

5.7. Orientation–aspect-ratio design map of the higher-order double stress
  Both α and AR set the boundary-layer intensity, so this subsection maps their combined
effect on the peak double shear stress max |τxyy|. Across 42 analyses the peak is extracted from
the solved displacement vector and normalized by µγ0a2.
  Figure 6 spans seven angles α ∈{0◦, 15◦, 30◦, 45◦, 60◦, 75◦, 90◦} and six aspect ratios AR ∈
{1, 2, 3, 5, 7, 10}. Two edges of the surface are flat at 0.4227: the isotropic limit (AR = 1,
all angles) and the horizontal-alignment edge (α = 0◦, all aspect ratios), since g222(0) = a22/5
regardless of AR. Away from those edges the peak climbs steeply. It reaches 7.7536 at (AR =

aligning an elongated domain with the boundary normal maximizes the non-local interaction

 The chart is a direct tuning tool. Low AR or α ≈0◦keeps higher-order stress small — the
regime for flexible substrates where interfacial compliance matters. High AR with α ≈90◦
maximizes boundary-layer stiffening — the regime for laminated joints or cellular struts that
need to resist localized shear.

6. Conclusions

  This work combined Mindlin Form-II strain-gradient elasticity with a C1-continuous Bogner–
Fox–Schmit finite element formulation to study how anisotropic ellipsoidal microstructures
affect the response of size-dependent solids under simple shear. The model uses an orientation-
dependent characteristic-length tensor, which lets the microstructural aspect ratio and orien-
tation be varied independently. The implementation was first checked against an established
analytical benchmark, with close quantitative agreement. The main findings follow.

   • The BFS formulation reproduces the analytical benchmark solution and retains the con-
     tinuous second-order derivatives that strain-gradient elasticity requires. It ran stably over
     the full parameter range without artificial stabilization.

   • Increasing the orientation angle α raises the boundary-normal characteristic length g222(α).
     Aligning the ellipsoid with the boundary normal strengthens the higher-order non-local in-
      teractions, produces larger double shear stresses, and thickens the strain-gradient bound-
     ary layers at the constrained interfaces.

   • The aspect ratio controls the spatial distribution of the higher-order internal forces.
     Highly elongated microstructures concentrate stress at the boundaries and amplify the
      non-classical effects throughout the domain.

   • The three-dimensional response surfaces and design maps trace the transition from clas-
       sical Cauchy elasticity to gradient-dominated behaviour, showing how aspect ratio and
      orientation act together.

   • The energy-partition analysis shows stored mechanical energy shifting from the classical

   • The boundary-layer thickness surface, polar phase diagram, and energy-partition maps

 The formulation is an efficient tool for anisotropic size-dependent solids and makes the link
between microstructural morphology and the higher-order mechanical fields explicit. It is suited
to structural design problems where boundary-layer behaviour and non-classical stress distri-
butions matter — architected materials, lattice structures, and composite systems being the
natural cases.
