                                      Abstract

          Flexoelectricity couples electric polarization to strain gradients and, unlike piezoelectricity, is
      permitted by symmetry in every dielectric, so it becomes a candidate transduction mechanism pre-
       cisely at sub-micrometre scales. The governing equations of a coupled piezoelectric–flexoelectric
       solid with Mindlin Form-II strain-gradient elasticity form a fourth-order electromechanical sys-
     tem whose conforming discretization requires C1 continuity and whose raw stiffness matrix spans
     some twenty orders of magnitude. This paper develops a conforming Bogner–Fox–Schmit finite
      element formulation for this coupled problem in plane strain, adopting a shear-free flexoelectric
       specialization and applying a complete non-dimensionalization as a symmetric congruence that
      improves the conditioning by more than twenty orders of magnitude without altering the physical
      solution. The two-mesh rates computed from the source-reported Shekarchizadeh norms are
      3.9998, 2.9963 and 1.9995, consistent with the theoretical orders 4, 3 and 2; the adopted PZT-5H
      constants reproduce the IEEE Std 176 analytical chain (k31 = 0.3887 against the tabulated 0.39,
      Eopen = 71.38 GPa, Timoshenko tip deflection 5.65×10−14 m) and the electromechanical energy
       identities to a relative residual of order 10−12. Studies of a barium titanate cantilever reveal a
       self-stiffening saturation of the open-circuit potential, a critical thickness scaling hcrit ∝ℓ0.90
      obtained by an empirical power-law fit to four source-reported critical-thickness roots, and an
      energy conversion ratio that decreases with slenderness in the reduced model, traced to the
      absence of the transverse bending coupling rather than to any computational defect. The results
      delineate which size-dependent responses the shear-free model captures and which require the
        full flexoelectric tensor.


Keywords: flexoelectricity; piezoelectricity; strain-gradient elasticity; Mindlin Form-II; Bogner–
Fox–Schmit finite element; C1 continuity; electromechanical coupling; size effect.


1  Introduction

Electromechanical coupling in dielectric solids underpins a wide range of sensors, actuators, and
energy-conversion devices. In classical piezoelectricity, electric polarization is coupled directly to

strain. The constitutive framework developed for piezoelectric ceramics has long provided the basis

for modelling piezoelectric transducers and structures IEEE Standard on Piezoelectricity [1988], Jaffe


                                          1

--- PAGE 2 ---
et al. [1971], Tiersten [1969], and distributed-parameter models of piezoelectric beams and plates

remain widely used in structural sensing and energy harvesting Erturk and Inman [2011], Preumont

[2011]. This framework, however, has two limitations that become important at small length scales.
Piezoelectricity requires a non-centrosymmetric material symmetry, and the classical constitutive

equations contain no intrinsic material length scale. Consequently, the electromechanical response of

a linear piezoelectric solid does not acquire an additional size dependence unless a length-dependent
mechanism is introduced.

    Flexoelectricity provides such a mechanism by coupling electric polarization to strain gradients.

Unlike piezoelectricity, flexoelectricity is permitted by symmetry in dielectric materials more gener-
ally Sharma et al. [2010], Tagantsev [1986]. Because strain gradients increase as the characteristic
structural dimension decreases, flexoelectric effects can become important in thin films, nanowires,
and micro- and nano-scale components Wang et al. [2019], Zubko et al. [2013]. Early theoretical
estimates suggested relatively small flexoelectric coefficients, but subsequent measurements in
relaxor ferroelectrics and PZT ceramics reported coefficients in the µC/m range Cross [2006],
Ma and Cross [2001]. Experiments on polymer films and ferroelectric thin films provided further
evidence of strain-gradient-induced polarization and its role in nanoscale electromechanical be-
haviour Baskaran et al. [2011], Catalan et al. [2011]. Atomistic calculations subsequently provided

estimates of flexoelectric coefficients and clarified their tensorial structure Maranganti and Sharma
[2009]. Continuum and atomistic studies also showed that flexoelectricity can produce appreciable
size-dependent electromechanical behaviour in nanostructures and composite systems Majdoub
et al. [2008], Zhu et al. [2006]. These developments established flexoelectricity as an important
size-dependent electromechanical mechanism.

   The mechanical framework needed to describe strain-gradient effects has a long history. Couple-
stress and generalized continuum theories introduced higher-order mechanical measures into the
elastic energy Koiter [1964], Mindlin and Tiersten [1962], Toupin [1962]. Mindlin subsequently
developed higher-order elasticity theories involving material length scales Mindlin [1964, 1965],
and the first strain-gradient formulation of Mindlin and Eshel provided a systematic constitutive

framework for isotropic materials Mindlin and Eshel [1968]. Germain placed generalized continuum

theories on a variational footing through the method of virtual power Germain [1973]. Experimental
studies later demonstrated the mechanical significance of internal length scales in bending and

torsion Lam et al. [2003], while simplified gradient and couple-stress theories extended the approach

to a range of engineering problems Aifantis [1992], Altan and Aifantis [1997], Ru and Aifantis [1993],
Yang et al. [2002]. Broader discussions of gradient elasticity, including constitutive formulations,
length-scale identification, and finite element implementation, are available in the literature Askes
and Aifantis [2011], dell’Isola et al. [2009].
   The constitutive description of flexoelectricity introduces an additional tensorial structure. For

cubic crystals, the flexoelectric tensor can be described by three independent coefficients associated
with longitudinal, transverse, and shear contributions, whereas isotropic symmetry imposes an

additional relation between these coefficients Le Quang and He [2011], Shu et al. [2011]. Continuum
formulations have incorporated flexoelectric coupling together with surface effects Shen and Hu
[2010], and beam and plate models have been developed to study size-dependent piezoelectric and

flexoelectric responses Liang et al. [2014], Yan and Jiang [2013]. The potential of flexoelectricity for
nanoscale energy conversion and harvesting has also been investigated Deng et al. [2014], Moura
and Erturk [2017]. Reviews of the subject have highlighted the generality of the effect while also

--- PAGE 3 ---
identifying unresolved issues in its constitutive description and computational treatment Gupta
et al. [2025], Wang et al. [2019], Yudin and Tagantsev [2013], Zubko et al. [2013].

   These constitutive developments have created a corresponding challenge for numerical methods.
Because strain-gradient elasticity introduces higher-order mechanical derivatives, conforming dis-
placement approximations require continuity beyond the C0 level. Early finite element treatments
of flexoelectricity therefore employed smooth meshfree approximations Abdollahi et al. [2014,

2015]. Isogeometric formulations subsequently provided a natural route to the required smoothness
and were extended to topology optimisation, sensitivity analysis, uncertainty quantification, and

immersed spline formulations Codony et al. [2019], Ghasemi et al. [2017, 2018], Hamdia et al. [2018],
Nanthakumar et al. [2017]. The mathematical and computational structure of flexoelectricity,

including the relation between direct and converse formulations, was further examined by Codony et
al. Codony et al. [2021]. For strain-gradient elasticity without electromechanical coupling, several
C1, mixed, and higher-order finite element formulations are available Adini and Clough [1961],
Amanatidou and Aravas [2002], Bogner et al. [1966], Engel et al. [2002], Papanicolopulos et al.
[2009], Petera and Pittman [1994], Shu et al. [1999], Zervos [2008], Zervos et al. [2009], together
with isogeometric formulations of gradient elasticity Fischer et al. [2011], Rudraraju et al. [2014].
A benchmark two-dimensional strain-gradient solution specifically intended for computational
verification was reported by Shekarchizadeh, Abali, and Bersani Shekarchizadeh et al. [2022].
   Despite these developments, the fully coupled piezoelectric–flexoelectric problem presents two

practical numerical issues that need to be addressed together. The discrete system combines elastic,
strain-gradient, coupling, and dielectric contributions with markedly different characteristic scales.

For slender structures, the derivative-related mechanical degrees of freedom introduce an additional

disparity, making the resulting saddle-point system severely ill-conditioned in its unscaled form. A
suitable scaling strategy is therefore needed to improve numerical conditioning without changing
the underlying physical solution. At the same time, an energy-based measure of electromechanical
conversion requires careful distinction between the dead-load product, the stored internal energy,
and the quasi-static work. These issues are not independent numerical details: they arise directly

from the higher-order coupled formulation and must remain consistent with its variational structure.
   The present work develops a conforming C1 Bogner–Fox–Schmit finite element formulation for
coupled piezoelectric–flexoelectric solids with Mindlin Form-II strain-gradient elasticity in plane
strain. The model adopts a reduced flexoelectric specialization in which the shear coefficient is set

to zero, and this specialization is stated explicitly so that the resulting predictions are distinguished

from those of the full flexoelectric tensor. The formulation is written from the electric enthalpy and

discretized using bicubic Hermite interpolation for the mechanical field and a bilinear approximation
for the electric potential. A consistent non-dimensionalization is introduced at the discrete level
as a symmetric congruence transformation to improve the conditioning of the coupled system
while preserving its symmetric saddle-point structure. The formulation is examined through a
strain-gradient benchmark, an independent piezoelectric analytical check, electromechanical energy
identities, and numerical studies of a barium titanate cantilever. Particular attention is given to
the flexoelectric figure of merit, slenderness, higher-order boundary conditions, and the critical
thickness at which strain-gradient effects become significant. The resulting analysis also makes
explicit which size-dependent responses are captured by the adopted shear-free model and which
depend on the omitted transverse flexoelectric coupling.
   The main contributions of this work are as follows:

2  Mathematical formulation

2.1  Geometry and plane-strain assumptions

The reference configuration is a bounded two-dimensional domain Ω⊂R2 with piecewise-smooth
boundary Γ, referred to Cartesian coordinates x = [x1, x2]T ≡[x, y]T. Plane strain is assumed
throughout,
                                                          ∂(·)
                                  u3(x1, x2) ≡0,      = 0,                                 (1)
                                              ∂x3
so that every field is a function of (x, y) only and the out-of-plane strain components vanish,
ε13 = ε23 = ε33 = 0. The in-plane displacement field is required to lie in the second-order Sobolev
space,
                          u = [u, v]T ∈[H2(Ω)]2,                                    (2)

because the strain-gradient energy contains second derivatives of u. Thus, the displacement field

and its weak derivatives up to second order must be square-integrable for the corresponding energy
to remain finite. This regularity requirement is the root of the C1-continuity condition that governs
the finite element discretisation developed in Section 3.

2.4  Electric field

The electric potential ϕ ∈H1(Ω) is the single electrical unknown, and the quasi-static electric field
is its negative gradient,
                                      Ei = −ϕ,i,                                          (8)

so that ∇× E = 0 identically. Quasi-electrostatics is assumed throughout, so magnetic induction
and electromagnetic wave effects are neglected and the electric field is determined from the scalar
potential at each mechanical state.

2.8  Reduced piezoelectric coupling

The two-dimensional plane-strain reduction of the piezoelectric tensor has the non-zero components

                           e211 = e31,     e222 = e33,     e112 = e121 = e15,                     (19)

with all other ekij = 0. These are the standard components entering the polarization and stress of
a transversely isotropic piezoelectric in the (x, y) plane IEEE Standard on Piezoelectricity [1988],
Tiersten [1969].


2.9  Reduced flexoelectric specialization

The reduced flexoelectric constitutive specialization adopted in the present model retains the
longitudinal and transverse components and sets the shear components to zero,

                       µ1111 = µ2222 = µ11,     µ1122 = µ2211 = µ12 + 2µ44,
                                                                                            (20)
                                  all other µlijk = 0,

together with the isotropic consistency relation

                                      µ11 = µ12 + 2µ44.                                    (21)

Two remarks place this specialization in context. First, the full isotropic (or cubic) flexoelectric tensor
possesses three independent components: the longitudinal coefficient µ1111 = µ11, the transverse
coefficient µ1122 = µ12, and the shear-bending components µ1212 = µ1221 = µ2112 = µ2121 = µ44
Le Quang and He [2011], Shu et al. [2011]. Setting µ44 = 0 collapses that tensor to (20) with
µ1122 = µ12 = µ11 whenever the consistency relation (21) holds, as it does for the benchmark values
µ11 = µ12 = 1.0 µC/m used below. The neglect of the shear coefficient follows the practice of
Abdollahi et al. Abdollahi et al. [2014], who retain only the longitudinal and transverse coefficients

omits the transverse bending component µ2112 of the full tensor. The consequences of this omission
for the size-dependent response are examined quantitatively in Section 5; it must not be conflated
with the full-tensor physics.


2.10  Governing equations

Stationarity of the total electromechanical potential with respect to independent variations of u
and ϕ yields the mechanical equilibrium equation and Gauss’s law,

                                σij,j −hijk,jk + fi = 0  in Ω,      Di,i = 0  in Ω,                    (22)

where fi is the body force and the negative sign of the double divergence hijk,jk is the signature of
the strain-gradient contribution Germain [1973], Mindlin and Eshel [1968]. Because hijk contains
second derivatives of u, the mechanical equation is of fourth order in the displacement. The electrical
equation is of second order in ϕ and couples to the mechanical field through the piezoelectric and
flexoelectric polarization.

    Substituting the constitutive relations and expanding the double divergence term by term gives
the explicit two-dimensional system. Introducing the coefficient combinations

throughout the numerical studies and prevent any ambiguity in the factor of two between work

3.2  Requirements on the approximation

The fourth-order mechanical operators in (25)–(26) demand, for a conforming Galerkin approxima-
tion, a displacement field whose first derivatives are continuous across element boundaries. The
weak form contains second derivatives of the test and trial functions, so a conforming space must
lie in H2(Ω). Classical C0 elements, whose interpolation is continuous but whose derivatives are
not, cannot represent the double stress consistently without auxiliary or mixed variables. Two
established routes exist: the mixed or discontinuous treatments reviewed in the introduction
Amanatidou and Aravas [2002], Engel et al. [2002], Zervos [2008], and directly C1-continuous
elements. The present work follows the second route with the Bogner–Fox–Schmit (BFS) rectangle
Bogner et al. [1966], whose bicubic Hermite interpolation guarantees interelement continuity of
both the field and its first derivatives on structured rectangular meshes, the natural discretisation
for the cantilever and plate geometries studied here.

3.5  Numerical integration

Each integrand in (55) is a product of two shape-function derivatives. The bicubic Hermite
functions have degree three in each variable, so first derivatives have degree at most three and
second derivatives degree at most three as well. The binding integrands BTη DVoigtBη and BTe CBe
therefore have degree at most six in each of ξ and η. A Gauss–Legendre rule with n points per
direction integrates polynomials of degree up to 2n −1 exactly, and

                            2n −1 = 2 · 4 −1 = 7 ≥6,                               (57)

so the 4 × 4 tensor-product rule integrates every element integral to machine precision, limited only
by round-off rather than by quadrature error.

3.7  Solution and recovery

The element matrices of (55) are evaluated with the 4×4 Gauss rule, assembled over the structured
mesh, and constrained by the essential boundary conditions of Section 2.12. The constrained
system is scaled through (60) and solved by a direct method for the symmetric indefinite saddle
system Benzi et al. [2005], Golub and Van Loan [2013]. The physical fields are then recovered
from the scaled solution, and all reported quantities—displacements, potential, energies, and the
conversion ratio—are computed from the recovered physical solution rather than from the scaled
representation.

4.8  Mesh convergence of the coupled problem

The approximation property examined in Figure 2(a) is a two-mesh, source-reported check on a
pure strain-gradient plate; it does not by itself certify the resolution of the coupled clamp layer.
Figure 3 therefore documents the mesh convergence of the two observables on which the results of
Section 5 rest: a benchmark point value and the baseline sensor response.

    Panel (a) shows the convergence of the simple-shear free-edge displacement under the refinement
12 × 4, 24 × 8, 48 × 16. The successive differences give the Richardson point-value rate p = 1.82
towards the extrapolated value u∗= 1.6988 × 10−9 m. That number is a functional rate of the
present solver, not an error-norm rate, and is reported separately from the quoted rates of Table 1.
   Panel (b) shows the tip deflection of the coupled baseline sensor under three refinement paths.
Combined refinement raises vtip monotonically from 2.52 × 10−16 m (8 × 1) through 2.69 × 10−16 m
(16 × 2) and 2.77 × 10−16 m (32 × 4) to 2.80 × 10−16 m (64 × 8), the last step changing the value
by 1.1%. Through-thickness refinement at Nx = 16 plateaus at 2.75 × 10−16 m (a 0.05% change
from Ny = 8 to Ny = 12), longitudinal refinement at Ny = 2 saturates near 2.73 × 10−16 m, and
the 60 × 12 mesh of the slenderness sweep reproduces the finest combined value to 0.03%. The
converged deflection is therefore vtip ≈2.80 × 10−16 m, and the 16 × 2 baseline mesh carries a
discretization offset of about 4% that is common to all solves on that mesh.
   The electrical clamp-layer quantities converge more slowly, because the retained flexoelectric
source D1 = µ11u,xx is concentrated in a through-thickness layer at the root. At L/h = 20 the
dielectric energy falls monotonically with Ny, from 2.37×10−27 J (16×2) to 9.2×10−28 J (Ny ≥8),
and the peak potential decreases from 2.96 × 10−10 V on the baseline mesh to 1.90 × 10−10 V on
the 60 × 12 export mesh. The conversion ratio computed on an Ny = 12 mesh changes by 2–4%
when Ny is reduced to 8 up to L/h = 30, and the change grows to 11% at L/h = 40 and 23%
at L/h = 60 (h = 33 nm), where the through-thickness resolution of the clamp layer becomes
the controlling discretization factor. These numbers are the resolution caveat attached to the
slender-range exponent of Section 5.4: ηEM is mesh-converged at L/h = 20 but only bracketed at
the largest slenderness, which is why the exponent is quoted with a confidence interval and is not
offered as a mesh-converged universal constant.

5.9  Physical interpretation of the reduced constitutive model

The two central observations of this study—the saturation of the potential and the negative slender-
ness exponent of the conversion ratio—share a single physical origin: the shear-free specialization
(20) removes the transverse bending polarization of the full flexoelectric tensor. In the full tensor the
bending strain gradient u,xy polarizes the beam uniformly through its thickness, and the resulting
surface charge is the classical flexoelectric response that grows with slenderness. In the reduced
tensor only the longitudinal polarization D1 = µ11u,xx survives. It is antisymmetric through the
thickness, and its volume divergence vanishes in a tip-loaded beam. The residual response is confined

to a boundary layer near the clamp, whose contribution to the stored dielectric energy decreases as
the beam becomes more slender. The same specialization underlies the corner-moment actuator
mechanism (67): with µ44 = 0 and a uniform transverse field the double stress is constant, so
actuation occurs only through the corner forces. These consequences are properties of the adopted
model, not of the discretisation. They delimit, quantitatively, the size-dependent phenomena that
a shear-free flexoelectric model can and cannot resolve.


5.10  Limitations and model scope

The present formulation is restricted to plane strain on structured rectangular meshes, which is the

natural setting of the BFS element and of the benchmark configurations studied. General geometries
require either isoparametric Hermite mappings Petera and Pittman [1994] or the immersed and
isogeometric alternatives Codony et al. [2019], Fischer et al. [2011]. The constitutive model retains
a single flexoelectric coefficient pair with µ44 = 0, so the quantitative predictions are those of the
shear-free limit, and the clamp-layer electrical quantities (peak potential, conversion ratio at large
slenderness) carry the through-thickness resolution caveats quantified in Section 4.8 and in the
refined figure-of-merit sweep of Section 5.3. Extending the formulation to the full three-parameter
isotropic tensor is a natural continuation, and the conditioning and energy machinery developed

comparisons remain the only quantities that cannot be recomputed from the material presented.


6  Conclusions

A conforming C1 Bogner–Fox–Schmit finite element formulation has been developed for coupled
piezoelectric–flexoelectric solids with Mindlin Form-II strain-gradient elasticity in plane strain.
The formulation spans the complete chain from the electric enthalpy and the five Mindlin–Eshel
invariants to the explicit fourth-order electromechanical strong form, the boundary operators
including the double traction and corner forces, and the symmetric indefinite saddle system of the
discretisation.

   The constitutive model adopts a reduced flexoelectric specialization in which the shear coefficient
is set to zero, following the established practice for barium titanate benchmarks, and states this
specialization explicitly. The discretisation uses the bicubic Hermite interpolation of the BFS
element, which provides exactly the C1 continuity demanded by the fourth-order mechanical
operators, with 4 × 4 Gauss–Legendre integration that is exact for every integrand.
  A complete non-dimensionalization of the field variables, applied as a symmetric congruence
transformation, reduces the condition number of the constrained coupled system by more than
twenty orders of magnitude on slender meshes while leaving the physical solution invariant to
within a relative residual of 3.5 × 10−13. The energy bookkeeping distinguishes the dead-load
product P = 2Uint from the quasi-static work Wqs = 12P, and the conversion ratio is defined on the
dead-load product consistently.
   The two-mesh rates computed from the source-reported Shekarchizadeh norms are 3.9998, 2.9963
and 1.9995, consistent with the theoretical orders of the bicubic element; the adopted PZT-5H
constants reproduce the IEEE Std 176 analytical chain (k31 = 0.3887 against the tabulated 0.39,
Eopen = 71.38 GPa, tip deflection 5.65 × 10−14 m) and the electromechanical energy identities to a
relative residual of order 10−12.

   The size-dependent response of the barium titanate cantilever exhibits a self-stiffening saturation
of the open-circuit potential at Λ of order 10−2; the rise–maximum–fall non-monotonicity persists
under through-thickness refinement from 16 × 2 to 60 × 12 elements, while the quantitative peak
values carry the resolution caveats of Section 4.8. The same studies yield a near-linear critical-
thickness law hcrit ≈24 ℓfitted to four source-reported roots, and an energy conversion ratio that
decreases with slenderness in the reduced model. The last observation is traced to the absence of
the transverse bending polarization in the shear-free specialization, and it demarcates the physical
regime in which the full flexoelectric tensor is required.

   The practical significance of the work is twofold. For device analysis, the formulation provides a

verified and numerically tractable computational basis for piezoelectric–flexoelectric cantilever and
plate problems, with the critical thickness law supplying a direct design rule. For model selection,
the work quantifies precisely which size-dependent phenomena are captured by the shear-free limit
and which are not, so that the constitutive specialization can be chosen deliberately rather than
implicitly.

  A natural extension is the incorporation of the full three-parameter isotropic flexoelectric tensor,
for which the discretisation, the conditioning treatment, and the energy machinery developed here
carry over unchanged. The diagnostic transverse-coupling study reported in this work already
