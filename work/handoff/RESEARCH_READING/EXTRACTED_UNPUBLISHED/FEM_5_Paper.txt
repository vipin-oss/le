                                      Abstract

          Pyroelectric crystals are necessarily also piezoelectric, so a spatially non-uniform temper-
      ature field drives both a direct pyroelectric response and an indirect piezoelectric response
     mediated by thermal strain. We present a variational finite-element model, based on a single
      electric-enthalpy potential and a conforming C1 Bogner–Fox–Schmit element, for the open-
       circuit voltage of a plane-strain tetragonal BaTiO3 microcantilever under a steady through-
      thickness temperature difference. For a tetragonal crystal, the plane-strain thermal-stress vec-
      tor must be obtained by contracting the full three-dimensional thermal-expansion and elastic
      tensors before the plane-strain constraint is imposed; a simplified reduction that omits the con-
      strained transverse thermal expansion under-predicts the resulting thermal-stress components
     by 35–58% and, for the present geometry and loading, reverses the sign of the piezoelectric-
      thermal contribution to the open-circuit voltage. We also distinguish the fixed-strain (primary)
      pyroelectric coefficient required by the strain-based constitutive law from the free (total) co-
       efficient ordinarily reported from bulk measurements, taking the primary coefficient from a
       first-principles calculation and the anisotropic thermal-expansion coefficients from regression
       of single-crystal diffraction data. On a mesh-converged discretization, the pyroelectric-only,
       piezoelectric-plus-pyroelectric, and piezoelectric-plus-thermal open-circuit voltages are approx-
      imately −3.4 V, −1.5 V, and +0.19 V for a 10 µm × 1 µm specimen under a 10 K temperature
       difference. The polarity of the piezoelectric-thermal voltage is robust to the propagated un-
      certainty of the thermal-expansion regression at one standard deviation, reversing only beyond
      approximately two standard deviations on the transverse coefficient, while the pyroelectric-only
      voltage is sensitive to the uncertain clamped-versus-free classification of the transverse dielectric
      constant. We did not identify an experimental full-device voltage measurement for direct com-
      parison. An optional flexoelectric extension is retained in the formulation but excluded from
      the quantitative results, since neither an effective flexoelectric tensor nor an internal length
       scale is presently available for this material.

Keywords:  pyroelectricity; piezoelectricity; barium titanate; thermal expansion; finite element

method; Bogner–Fox–Schmit element; plane strain.

1  Introduction

Pyroelectric materials convert a change in temperature directly into an electrical response through

the temperature dependence of their spontaneous polarization, a property exploited in infrared

detectors, thermal imaging, and thermal energy-harvesting devices that convert ambient or waste-

heat temperature fluctuations into electrical power [Lang, 2005, Whatmore, 1986, Lubomirsky

and Stafsudd, 2012]. A microcantilever is a natural geometry for such a device:  it is compliant

enough that a modest thermal gradient across its thickness produces a mechanically significant

bending response, and cantilever geometries of this kind are widely used as piezoelectric energy-

harvesting test structures, so a model that couples pyroelectric and piezoelectric response in this

geometry addresses both a materials-science question (how the two mechanisms combine) and a

device-engineering one (what open-circuit voltage a given specimen and thermal load can deliver).

Because every pyroelectric crystal class is necessarily also piezoelectric – pyroelectricity requires a

unique polar axis, a symmetry restriction that is a subset of the non-centrosymmetry piezoelectricity

requires – a spatially non-uniform temperature field in a real pyroelectric device drives both the

pyroelectric coefficient directly and, through the resulting non-uniform thermal strain, an indirect

piezoelectric contribution. Separating and quantifying these two contributions, together with any

constraint-induced amplification or cancellation from a specific device geometry, requires a fully

coupled thermo-piezo-electromechanical boundary-value treatment rather than an estimate based

on either mechanism in isolation. The open-circuit voltage is the natural quantity to compute

and report for this purpose:  it is the maximum potential difference the specimen can sustain

under a given thermal load without drawing current, is directly comparable across specimens and

mechanisms independent of any external load resistance, and is the quantity a static or quasi-static

electrostatic voltmeter measurement would access most directly.

  A conventional treatment of such a device often estimates the two contributions separately:

the pyroelectric voltage from a one-dimensional, purely electrical argument, and the piezoelectric

contribution, if considered at all, from a simplified thermal-stress estimate that does not track the

full anisotropy of the crystal’s thermal-expansion tensor. Section 5 shows quantitatively that this

simplification is not conservative: for the clamped-cantilever geometry considered here, it does not

merely change the piezoelectric-thermal voltage’s magnitude but can invert its sign, so a model

that is only qualitatively aware of the coupling is insufficient to predict even the polarity of the

device response.  Thermodynamically coupled formulations that incorporate temperature as an

independent field alongside strain and electric field – thermopiezoelectricity – were developed by

Mindlin and by Nowacki [Mindlin, 1972, Nowacki, 1975] and remain the appropriate starting point

for a device model, such as the present one, in which a prescribed temperature field is the load.
   Barium titanate (BaTiO3) is a natural test material for such a study:  its room-temperature
tetragonal phase has a complete, independently measured set of elastic, piezoelectric, and dielectric

constants [Zgonik et al., 1994, Berlincourt and Jaffe, 1958], and its sequence of ferroelectric phase

transitions and associated symmetry changes were established in classical single-crystal studies

[Merz, 1949, Kay and Vousden, 1949] and rationalized thermodynamically by Devonshire’s phe-

nomenological theory [Devonshire, 1949, 1951]. It is also thermally anisotropic: the a- and c-axis

lattice parameters of the tetragonal cell respond to temperature with opposite sign, confirmed by

modern variable-temperature diffraction [Nakatani et al., 2016] and consistent with independent

thermal-expansion measurements on related barium titanate-based ceramics [Han et al., 2015, He,

2004].  Propagating this anisotropic expansion into a mechanical boundary-value problem uses

standard thermoelastic formalism [Boley and Weiner, 1997, Nowacki, 1975], but its plane-strain

specialization for a tetragonal crystal is not entirely routine: a reduction that discards the con-

strained transverse thermal expansion before forming the full three-dimensional thermal-stress con-

traction misstates the thermal forcing available to drive both the mechanical and the electrical

response, as developed in Section 2.3. This is precisely the kind of modeling shortcut that a purely

one-dimensional or isotropic treatment of the thermal-expansion tensor would not expose, since

the transverse component only enters through the off-diagonal elastic constants that couple the

in-plane directions.

  A second material-science point concerns the pyroelectric coefficient itself. Pyroelectricity de-

composes into a primary contribution at fixed strain and a secondary contribution arising from

thermal expansion acting through the piezoelectric effect in a mechanically free sample; their sum

is the total (free) coefficient ordinarily reported from bulk measurements [Lang, 2005, Tagantsev,

1991, Lubomirsky and Stafsudd, 2012]. A strain-based electric-enthalpy formulation, in which

electric displacement is written as a function of total strain, field, and temperature, requires the

primary coefficient: the secondary contribution is already generated self-consistently by the piezo-

electric term acting on the solved strain field, so using the total coefficient would double-count it.

This distinction is easy to overlook in a coupled model, because the total coefficient is the one most

commonly tabulated for ceramics, while the primary coefficient required by the constitutive law is

only available from a more specialized measurement or calculation. A recent first-principles study
of tetragonal BaTiO3 reports primary, secondary, and total pyroelectric coefficients from one inter-
nally consistent calculation [Eklund and Karttunen, 2023], providing a directly traceable primary

coefficient in place of an inherited ceramic total coefficient measured under different conditions.

    Flexoelectricity, the coupling of polarization to strain gradient rather than to strain itself, is

symmetry-allowed in every dielectric – unlike piezoelectricity, it does not require a non-centrosymmetric

crystal class – and has been widely studied as a size-dependent mechanism that persists where piezo-

electricity vanishes and typically strengthens as the relevant length scale shrinks toward the inter-

nal material length scale, making it particularly relevant to the thin, high-curvature bending fields

present in microcantilever geometries [Tagantsev, 1986, Cross, 2006, Zubko et al., 2013], including

in barium titanate itself [Ma and Cross, 2006] and in microcantilever geometries directly analogous

to the one considered here [Huang et al., 2011, Mao et al., 2016, Abdollahi et al., 2014]. A physi-

cally consistent flexoelectric strain-gradient term requires an internal material length scale, whose

theoretical basis rests on the strain-gradient continuum theories of Toupin and Mindlin [Toupin,

1962, Mindlin, 1964, Mindlin and Eshel, 1968]. No experimentally justified length scale or effective
flexoelectric tensor is presently available for the tetragonal BaTiO3 system considered here; the
flexoelectric extension is retained in the governing formulation for completeness but excluded from

the quantitative results, with the supporting mesh-convergence evidence reported in Section 6.

  A coupled problem of this kind admits no simple closed-form solution once realistic boundary

conditions are imposed:  the clamped-cantilever constraint makes the strain field spatially non-

uniform in a way that a one-dimensional or lumped-parameter model cannot capture, and it is ex-

actly this non-uniform strain, generated self-consistently with the electrical response, that this study

shows to control the sign of the piezoelectric-thermal contribution. The finite-element method is

the standard tool for such coupled-field boundary-value problems [Zienkiewicz et al., 2005, Hughes,

2000, Belytschko et al., 2000], and its dedicated piezoelectric formulation dates to Allik and Hughes

[Allik and Hughes, 1970], with subsequent two- and three-dimensional device-level implementations

[Lerch, 1990, Tzou and Tseng, 1990] and a review by Benjeddou [Benjeddou, 2000]; general FEM

procedures for coupled-field and multiphysics problems are standard in the computational mechan-

ics literature [Bathe, 1996]. A strain-gradient (flexoelectric) term, even when switched off in the
calculations reported here, requires a mechanical interpolation with C1 continuity of displacement

across element boundaries, provided here by the classical Bogner–Fox–Schmit (BFS) rectangular
element [Bogner et al., 1965] rather than a mixed or penalty-based C0 formulation, which would

introduce additional interface unknowns not otherwise required by this problem.

   Taken together, these observations identify a specific gap: existing thermopiezoelectric formu-
lations provide the governing equations, and existing BaTiO3 characterizations provide elastic,
piezoelectric, dielectric, thermal-expansion, and pyroelectric data from independent sources, but

the two have not previously been combined, for this material and geometry, with (i) the full three-

dimensional thermal-stress contraction correctly reduced to plane strain, and (ii) the thermody-

namically consistent primary pyroelectric coefficient rather than an inherited total coefficient. The

present study addresses this gap directly, using a verified finite-element implementation to quantify

the sensitivity of the resulting open-circuit voltage, including its polarity, to both material-input

choices.

   This paper (i) derives the plane-strain thermal-stress vector for a tetragonal crystal from the

full three-dimensional thermal-expansion contraction and shows that an earlier, simplified reduction

not only under-predicts its magnitude but can reverse the sign of the resulting open-circuit voltage;

(ii) uses the fixed-strain (primary) pyroelectric coefficient required by the constitutive law, sourced

from a first-principles calculation, in place of a total (free) coefficient inherited from an unrelated

ceramic measurement; (iii) sources the anisotropic thermal-expansion coefficients from single-crystal

diffraction data, with the fitting uncertainty reported and propagated; (iv) verifies the finite-element

implementation against analytical and algebraic identities; (v) reports mesh-converged open-circuit

voltages for three physically distinct cases together with a sensitivity analysis; and (vi) states,

without resolving, the limitation that the elastic, thermal-expansion, and pyroelectric inputs come

from more than one independent source.

2.4  Coupled higher-order (flexoelectric) formulation

An effective flexoelectric term, Dµi = µijklηjkl with ηjkl = εjk,l the strain-gradient tensor, and an
accompanying Mindlin Form-II-type strain-gradient elastic stiffness A = C44ℓ2I with ℓan internal
material length scale, are present in the general formulation and are implemented reciprocally (i.e.,
as cross-derivatives of a single enthalpy augmented with 12Ajklmnoηjklηmno −µkjklηjklEk terms,
consistent with the strain-gradient continuum theories of Toupin, Mindlin, and Koiter [Toupin,

1962, Mindlin, 1964, Mindlin and Eshel, 1968] and their subsequent length-scale-identification and

engineering formulations [Fleck and Hutchinson, 1997, Lam et al., 2003, Askes and Aifantis, 2011]).

This extension is retained for generality and to document a reciprocal implementation consistent

with recent flexoelectric finite-element frameworks [Mao et al., 2016, Mao and Purohit, 2014, Ab-

dollahi et al., 2014], but it is not exercised in the results reported here: neither an independently
measured effective flexoelectric tensor µijkl – of the kind reported from first-principles or atomistic
calculations for other perovskite and crystalline dielectrics [Maranganti and Sharma, 2009, Hong

and Vanderbilt, 2011, Stengel, 2013] – nor a physically justified internal length scale ℓ, of the kind

measured directly in flexoelectric barium titanate and related ceramics [Ma and Cross, 2006, Huang
et al., 2011], is presently available for the specific single-crystal tetragonal BaTiO3 material system
considered here, and mesh studies of the flexoelectric-active configuration do not converge at any

of the mesh densities used for the non-flexoelectric cases (Section 6).

2.5  Boundary conditions

The cantilever is clamped at x = 0 (all mechanical degrees of freedom zero) and traction-free

elsewhere. The bottom face (z = 0) is a grounded electrode, ϕ = 0; the top face (z = h) is

a single floating equipotential surface with zero net charge (open circuit), enforced as a discrete

weak statement of total charge balance across all top-face electrical degrees of freedom, not as

a pointwise condition on the reconstructed field. A steady, linear through-thickness temperature

field is prescribed, θ(z) = ∆T z/h.  Figure 1 summarizes the specimen configuration, boundary

conditions, and the mechanism chain implemented by the solver, from the prescribed temperature

field through the corrected constitutive and coupling blocks to the reported open-circuit voltage.

the reduced system is solved with a direct sparse solver.

3.5  Assembly and numerical implementation

Each 32 + 4 element block is scattered into the global sparse system by the standard element

connectivity map [Bathe, 1996]. The resulting reduced algebraic system is solved after an exact
diagonal congruence scaling (K →S−1KS−1, q →Sq, f →S−1f, for a diagonal matrix S), an

exact change of variables that leaves the physical solution unchanged, to control the otherwise very

large condition number arising from mixing value-, slope-, and electrical-type degrees of freedom of

very different physical magnitude (typically metres, radians, and volts) in a single solution vector,

not because the physics is ill-posed.

of Section 2.3. The comparison illustrates that a free-sample estimate does not carry over to a

mechanically constrained geometry, and that the full coupled solution is required.


5.7  Discussion

The three cases isolate distinct physical origins of the open-circuit voltage. Case D reflects the pyro-
electric response alone and matches the exact one-dimensional identity V = p3¯θh/κ33 to 1.5×10−13
relative error, confirming that the discretization, sign convention, and open-circuit constraint are

correctly implemented; this agreement is a property of the numerical implementation and is inde-
pendent of whether p3 itself is the physically correct primary coefficient for BaTiO3, a distinction
discussed further below. Case E isolates the piezoelectric-thermal response: the clamp at x = 0

prevents the free thermal expansion that would otherwise occur, generating a non-uniform strain

field that, through the piezoelectric coupling, produces a voltage of opposite sign and substantially

smaller magnitude than the pyroelectric case.  Case A, combining both mechanisms in a single

coupled solve, does not equal the sum of Cases D and E, because Case D is computed with the

piezoelectric coupling switched offentirely (a different stiffness matrix), not because the underlying

system is nonlinear; for fixed material properties the system is linear in the applied thermal load,


                                          17

--- PAGE 18 ---
and this is confirmed directly by the exact affine dependence of Case A on the p3 scale factor
(Section 5).

  A simplified reduction that omits the constrained transverse thermal-expansion contribution
underestimates βxx by 35.05% and βzz by 57.75% relative to the full three-dimensional thermal-
stress contraction of Section 2.3, evaluated at the same thermal-expansion coefficients. Because
βPS enters both the mechanical load vector and, through the strain field it generates, the piezo-
electric contribution to the electrical response, this difference is not a minor detail: it alters every

reported voltage in Cases A and E (Case D is thermally decoupled from the mechanical solve and

is unaffected), and, as the following paragraph shows, changes the sign, not only the magnitude, of

Case E.


The central qualitative result of this work   is that the polarity of the piezoelectric-thermal

open-circuit voltage is a discriminator between the two constitutive reductions, not merely a matter
of degree. On a common 48 × 24 mesh, solving with the simplified (uncorrected) βPS gives VA =
−1.909285 V and VE = −0.215422 V, whereas the corrected reduction gives VA = −1.500906 V
and VE = +0.192957 V – a rigid shift of +0.4084 V common to both cases (Case D, which does
not depend on βPS, is unchanged to 0.000 × 100). The corrected, converged (80 × 40) value is
VE = +0.193211761 V (Table 4). A measurement of the sign of VE for a physical device of
this configuration would therefore directly test which thermal-stress reduction is closer to correct,

independently of any uncertainty in the absolute magnitude of the material inputs.


Robustness of this polarity to thermal-expansion uncertainty.  Because VE’s sign depends
on βPS, and βPS depends on the regression-derived α1, α3 (Section 4), it is necessary to check
whether the reported +0.19 V polarity survives the α regression’s own uncertainty rather than
being an artifact of the particular fitted values. Treating α1, α3 as independent (their regressions
use disjoint data, a(T) and c(T)) and propagating linearly through the mesh-converged solver

   The tension between the secondary-pyroelectric identity evaluated with the present room-

is a legitimate outcome of that difference in provenance rather than evidence that one dataset is

in error. This tension motivates future work sourcing a single, temperature-matched, single-crystal

dataset spanning all required properties (Section 6), but does not by itself invalidate the use of the

directly tabulated primary coefficient in the present model, since the primary coefficient carries no

such cross-dataset dependency.

5.8  Physical interpretation of the coupled response

We state here, in physical rather than purely numerical terms, why each case takes the sign and

order of magnitude that it does. Case D isolates the direct pyroelectric mechanism: the prescribed
temperature gradient θ(z) = ∆T z/h acts through the primary coefficient p3 alone, with no me-
chanical solve contributing to the electrical response at all (the piezoelectric block is switched off),

so the resulting voltage is simply the integrated pyroelectric charge divided by the clamped capac-
itance of the specimen, exactly the closed-form identity V = p3¯θh/κ33 that Section 5.4 confirms
to 1.5 × 10−13 relative accuracy.  Its magnitude is large, relative to the other two cases, simply
because p3 is itself the largest dimensional coefficient among the three mechanisms considered once
converted to consistent SI units, and because this mechanism requires no strain to be generated

at all – unlike Cases A and E, it does not compete with the mechanical stiffness of the clamped

structure for a share of the applied thermal energy.

   Case E isolates the purely mechanical route to an electrical response: the same temperature

field, now with the pyroelectric coefficient set to zero, produces a non-uniform thermal eigenstrain
αijθ that the clamp at x = 0 prevents the specimen from relieving by free expansion. This constraint
generates genuine elastic strain – most concentrated near the root, exactly where Figure 3(c) shows
εxx peaking – which then drives an electrical response through the converse-to-direct piezoelectric
coupling Dz = e31εxx + e33εzz integrated over the specimen and balanced against the open-circuit
condition. Because this mechanism requires the thermal eigenstrain to first be converted to elastic

strain by a mechanical constraint, and only then converted to charge by the piezoelectric tensor, it is
a two-step, indirect process, and it is unsurprising that its magnitude (≈0.19 V) is roughly an order
of magnitude smaller than the single-step direct pyroelectric mechanism of Case D (≈−3.4 V).
What is not self-evident, and is this paper’s central result, is that mechanism’s sign: because βPS
is a specific, sign-carrying linear combination of α1 and α3 (Section 2.3, Eq. (5)), and because
that combination mixes the (positive) a-axis and (negative) c-axis thermal expansion coefficients



                                          20

--- PAGE 21 ---
through elastic constants of comparable magnitude, the sign of Case E’s voltage is not fixed by

physical intuition alone – it must be computed from the full three-dimensional contraction, and,

as shown above, an incomplete contraction can invert this sign, not merely change its size. An

incomplete plane-strain reduction can therefore invert a polarity that would otherwise be treated

as a robust, experimentally checkable prediction.

   Case A, finally, combines both mechanisms in a single coupled solve rather than by superposition
(Section 5), and its magnitude (≈−1.5 V) sits between the pure pyroelectric value and zero precisely

because the mechanically generated (Case-E-like) contribution has the opposite sign to the dominant
pyroelectric contribution once the corrected βPS is used: the piezoelectric term partially cancels,
rather than reinforces, the pyroelectric term for this specimen and loading. This cancellation is itself

a physically meaningful, testable prediction, distinguishable in principle from a device in which the

two mechanisms reinforce one another, and it follows directly and only from the corrected sign of
βPS established in Section 2.3.


6  Limitations

 1. We did not identify an experimental full-device open-circuit-voltage measurement for direct

   comparison with Cases A, D, or E; the reported values are computational predictions, veri-

    fied internally against analytical and algebraic identities, but not compared against externally

   measured device data.

 2. The elastic, piezoelectric, and dielectric constants [Zgonik et al., 1994], the thermal-expansion

    coefficients [Nakatani et al., 2016], and the primary pyroelectric coefficient [Eklund and Kart-

   tunen, 2023] originate from three independent sources; while each is individually a primary,
    directly traceable measurement or calculation for tetragonal single-crystal BaTiO3, the combi-
   nation has not been validated as a single internally consistent dataset from one experiment.

 3. The α3 (c-axis) thermal-expansion regression has a coefficient of determination of only ≈0.47
    over the four fitted points nearest room temperature; the reported value is retained at two

    significant figures to reflect this. Within the propagated regression uncertainty (Section 5.7),
   the Case E polarity is robust at ±1σ and the Case A magnitude is robust at ±2σ; a higher-

    precision, dedicated near-room-temperature measurement would still narrow this uncertainty.

 4. The dielectric constants κ11, κ33 are transcribed without an independently verified clamped-
    versus-free classification; Section 4 shows the (e15, C44, κ11) triple is not fully self-consistent
   under either reading, with a ±0.5–0.7% sensitivity in Cases A/E and a substantially larger
    sensitivity in the Case D voltage to κ33’s uncertain range: the −3.4 V value should not be
   read as carrying the same robustness as the polarity result of Item 3. A dedicated, phase- and

   frequency-matched dielectric measurement would resolve this.

 5. The model represents the cantilever in plane strain, per unit out-of-plane depth, which is an

    idealization adopted because it removes one spatial dimension while retaining the anisotropic

    in-plane elastic and thermal-expansion coupling that is central to this study. The reported

    voltages apply to this plane-strain idealization and may differ for a finite-width cantilever,

   whose lateral surfaces permit mechanical constraints (and stress relaxation) that plane strain



                                          21

--- PAGE 22 ---
    excludes; extending the model to finite width would require re-solving the corresponding three-

   dimensional boundary-value problem.

 6. The flexoelectric extension to the model is implemented reciprocally but is not used to gener-

    ate quantitative predictions: no independently measured effective flexoelectric tensor and no

    physically justified strain-gradient length scale are presently available for this material. Mesh

    studies of the flexoelectric-active configurations (Cases B and C) solve stably and converge
   monotonically at all six densities studied, but their final-step relative changes, −1.4976% (B)
   and −2.1761% (C), remain one to two orders of magnitude above the 0.1% criterion satisfied by

   the non-flexoelectric cases; the flexoelectric results are therefore reported as exploratory only,

   and no quantitative claim in this paper rests on them.

 7. Discretization uncertainty for Cases A and E is estimated independently of the final-step cri-

    terion in Table 5, from the observed convergence order on the geometric mesh subsequence
   8 × 4 →16 × 8 →32 × 16 →64 × 32: the two successive-triplet order estimates are 1.355

   and 1.307 (Case A) and 1.458 and 1.526 (Case E), giving an estimated discretization error
   band of ±0.091% (A) and ±0.146% (E) at the 80 × 40 mesh, with an estimator-to-estimator

   spread of 0.174% and 0.275% respectively. These are observed rates on the meshes studied,

   not a claim about the element’s theoretical asymptotic order; a nonlinear least-squares fit of
  V∞+ Chp was also attempted but returned its initial guess for p regardless of the seed value
    tested (p0 ∈{2, 3, 4, 6}), indicating it is not usefully constrained by six data points spanning
    less than one order of magnitude in h, and its output is not used here.

 8. The reported voltages apply to the specific geometry and boundary conditions modeled here,

   and should not be extrapolated to other device configurations without re-solving the coupled

   system.


7  Conclusions

 1. The plane-strain thermal-stress vector for a tetragonal material must be derived from the full

    three-dimensional thermal-expansion contraction before the plane-strain constraint is applied; a

    simplified reduction that skips this step underestimates the resulting thermal-stress components
   by 35.05% (βxx) and 57.75% (βzz) for the present BaTiO3 parameters.

 2. The consequence for this specimen and loading is that the simplified reduction reverses the sign
    of the piezoelectric-thermal open-circuit voltage (VE: −0.22 V with the simplified reduction,
   +0.19 V with the full contraction) – the polarity, not only the magnitude, distinguishes which

    reduction was used.

 3. The pyroelectric coefficient entering a strain-based electric-enthalpy formulation must be the

    fixed-strain (primary) coefficient, not the free (total) coefficient ordinarily reported from bulk

   measurements; using a directly tabulated first-principles primary coefficient in place of an in-

    herited ceramic total coefficient changes the computed voltages substantially.

 4. On a mesh-converged 80×40 discretization, the pyroelectric-only, piezoelectric-plus-pyroelectric,
   and piezoelectric-plus-thermal open-circuit voltages for a 10 µm × 1 µm BaTiO3 cantilever in


                                          22

--- PAGE 23 ---
   plane strain under a 10 K temperature difference are approximately −3.4 V, −1.5 V, and

   +0.19 V, respectively. The polarity of the piezoelectric-thermal voltage is robust to the propa-

   gated thermal-expansion uncertainty at one standard deviation, but the pyroelectric-only mag-

   nitude depends on the uncertain clamped-versus-free classification of the transverse dielectric

   constant (Section 6) and should not be read as equally robust.

 5. The material inputs are assembled from three independent sources (elastic and dielectric con-

    stants, thermal-expansion coefficients, and the primary pyroelectric coefficient) rather than one

    internally consistent experiment, and a resulting tension between a room-temperature-tensor-

   based and a first-principles secondary-pyroelectric coefficient is reported rather than reconciled.

 6. An experimental measurement of the open-circuit voltage, and in particular of the sign of the

    piezoelectric-thermal contribution, would provide a direct test of the plane-strain thermal-stress

    reduction proposed here.
